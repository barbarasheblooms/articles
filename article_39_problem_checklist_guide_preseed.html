How to read the Pre-Seed problem checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Pre-Seed, the problem checklist looks like the easiest one to clear. It's usually the hardest to clear honestly, because every line can be technically true while still being too vague to convince anyone.

The Pre-Seed problem checklist has four lines. Each is short enough to tick without much thought, which is exactly why founders under-deliver on it more than any other round. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Problem statement clearly framed

This line is checking for specificity, not correctness. A problem statement is "clearly framed" when a stranger could repeat it back accurately after hearing it once: who has the problem, what the problem actually is, and why it happens. Being right about the problem isn't enough if it's stated in a way that could apply to a dozen different startups.

Common miss:
Describing the market or the industry ("small businesses struggle with cash flow") instead of naming a specific persona and a specific moment where the problem shows up for them.
What "done" looks like:
One sentence with three parts: persona, problem, root cause. "Independent hairdressers lose 2-3 client hours a week to no-shows because they book by text with no deposit system."
2
Backed by direct founder interviews (10+)

This line is checking two things at once: that the evidence came from you talking directly to people (not a survey you ran through someone else, and not secondhand accounts), and that there's enough volume for a pattern to actually exist. Ten is a floor, not a target. Below it, what you have is anecdote, not evidence.

Common miss:
Counting casual conversations with friends, advisors, or people who already know and like you as interviews, when they weren't structured and weren't with your actual target persona.
What "done" looks like:
A dated log: "14 interviews, [persona], conducted [date range], structured around [3-4 core questions]." If you can't produce that log on request, this line isn't actually done, even if you've had plenty of relevant conversations.
3
Interview quotes or secondary data included as evidence

This is the line most founders satisfy in their own head but not on the page. Having done the interviews is not the same as having surfaced evidence from them. This line is checking that a specific quote, statistic, or data point is actually visible in your materials, not just sitting in your notes.

Common miss:
Summarizing the interviews in your own words ("customers told us this was frustrating") without a single verbatim quote or number that an investor could point back to.
What "done" looks like:
At least one direct quote, attributed generically for privacy: "'I've just accepted losing that hour every week,' independent hairdresser, 6 years in business." Or a specific number: "9 of 14 interviewees had tried and abandoned a booking app in the last year."
4
Urgency explained: why now, not just why this problem

This line is checking for a distinction founders often collapse: explaining that a problem is painful is different from explaining why it needs solving now rather than being tolerated indefinitely, as it likely has been. "Why this problem" answers matter; "why now" answers are usually missing entirely.

Common miss:
Restating how bad the problem is, with more intensity, instead of naming a reason the tolerance for it is running out (a cost that's rising, a behavior that's shifting, an alternative that's disappearing).
What "done" looks like:
A one-line reason tied to your persona's actual situation: "No-show rates have risen since clients moved fully to text-based booking with no commitment step, a shift accelerated in the last two years."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Small businesses struggle with cash flow" (describes a market, not a persona)
  • "We talked to a lot of potential customers" (no count, no structure)
  • "Customers told us this was a real pain point" (no quote, no number)
  • "This problem is really bad for them" (intensity, not urgency)
Actually done
  • "Independent hairdressers lose 2-3 hours/week to no-shows because [root cause]"
  • "14 interviews, [persona], conducted over 3 weeks, structured questions"
  • "'I've just accepted losing that hour every week,' 6-year hairdresser"
  • "No-show rates rose after clients moved to text-only booking, in the last 2 years"

The pattern across all four: specificity beats intensity. A precise, narrow claim with a name, a number, or a quote attached does more work than a broader, more emphatic version of the same claim.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: could I show this to a stranger and have them point to the exact evidence? A persona they could describe back to you, an interview log they could ask to see, a quote they could read, a reason "now" that isn't just "always." If any answer is no, that line isn't done yet, however true it feels.

Key takeaways
  • Each checklist line is a compressed question; unpack what it's actually verifying before deciding it's done.
  • "Clearly framed" means a stranger could repeat back the persona, problem, and root cause accurately.
  • Ten interviews is a floor for a pattern to exist, and they must be direct and structured, not casual chats.
  • Evidence needs to be visible on the page: a real quote or number, not a paraphrase of what you heard.
  • Urgency means naming why the problem can't keep being tolerated, not just restating how painful it is.
