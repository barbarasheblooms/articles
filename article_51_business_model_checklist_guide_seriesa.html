How to read the Series A Business model checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Series A, the Business model checklist isn't asking whether the engine runs. It's asking whether the engine still runs the same way at three times the size, and what happens to the numbers when it doesn't.

The Series A Business model checklist has four lines. Each one is testing whether your economics are structurally sound enough to scale, not just healthy at your current size. This guide walks through all four: what each line is really verifying, the common way founders half-satisfy it, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Gross margin stated and benchmarked (60-70%+ SaaS expectation)

This line is checking whether you know your real gross margin, calculated the standard way (revenue minus cost of goods sold, including hosting, support, and any COGS-classified headcount), and whether you can place it against category benchmarks honestly. A margin below the expected range isn't automatically disqualifying, but an unexplained one is.

Common miss:
Quoting a "contribution margin" or a margin figure that excludes support costs or infrastructure spend, without labeling it as anything other than gross margin, which inflates the number against the benchmark.
What "done" looks like:
"Gross margin is 68%, in line with the 60-70% SaaS benchmark; the 4-point gap versus best-in-class peers is support staffing for our white-glove onboarding, which we expect to shrink to 2 points as self-serve onboarding ships in Q2."
2
Net revenue retention included

This line is checking for the single number that best predicts whether your growth compounds or has to be re-earned every quarter. NRR needs to be stated precisely, including the time window it covers and whether it's calculated at the account or the cohort level, since founders sometimes cherry-pick the strongest cohort to represent the whole base.

Common miss:
Reporting NRR from only the largest enterprise cohort, or from a single strong quarter, without disclosing that the blended NRR across all customer segments is meaningfully lower.
What "done" looks like:
"Blended NRR is 112% trailing twelve months; enterprise accounts run 128% NRR while SMB runs 96%, which is why our next hire is a dedicated SMB expansion rep."
3
Unit economics proven to scale, not just work today

This line is checking whether your CAC, margin, and payback period hold up as you move upmarket, add channels, or grow headcount, not whether they look fine at your current, still-small scale. The test is trend, not snapshot: are unit economics improving, flat, or degrading as volume increases, and why.

Common miss:
Presenting today's healthy CAC and payback as proof of scalability, with no cohort-over-cohort trend line showing whether those numbers hold, improve, or worsen as spend and headcount grow.
What "done" looks like:
"CAC has held between $380 and $410 across our last six sales hire cohorts despite tripling outbound headcount, and payback has improved from 14 to 10 months as our SDR ramp time shortened with better onboarding."
4
Pricing evolution with growth explained

This line is checking whether your pricing has actually changed as you've learned more about willingness to pay and moved into new segments, and whether you can explain that evolution as a deliberate strategy rather than a series of reactive discounts. Static pricing at Series A usually signals untapped margin, not stability.

Common miss:
Showing the same three pricing tiers used since launch with no mention of packaging changes, usage-based add-ons, or enterprise pricing introduced as the customer base has matured.
What "done" looks like:
"We introduced usage-based overage pricing 8 months ago and a dedicated enterprise tier 4 months ago; average revenue per account is up 34% year over year with less than 3% of that from new logo growth alone."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Contribution margin" quoted as gross margin, excluding real COGS
  • NRR reported from the strongest cohort only, blended figure hidden
  • Today's healthy CAC shown as proof, no cohort-over-cohort trend
  • Same three tiers since launch, no packaging or pricing evolution shown
Actually done
  • "68% gross margin, 4pt gap from support staffing, shrinking to 2pt in Q2" (labeled, explained)
  • "112% blended NRR; 128% enterprise, 96% SMB" (segmented, disclosed)
  • "CAC held $380-410 across 6 cohorts despite 3x headcount" (trend shown)
  • "Usage overage + enterprise tier added, ARPA up 34% YoY" (evolution, evidenced)

The pattern across all four: at Series A, the Business model checklist is testing durability under growth, meaning every metric needs to be shown as a trend across cohorts or segments, not as a single reassuring snapshot.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: if I tripled the underlying volume, would this number still hold, and can I show early evidence either way? A metric that's only ever been reported as a single point in time hasn't been tested for scale yet, it's just been observed once.

Key takeaways
  • Gross margin needs to be labeled honestly and benchmarked against the 60-70%+ SaaS range, with gaps explained.
  • NRR should be disclosed at the blended level and broken out by segment, not cherry-picked from the strongest cohort.
  • Unit economics need a cohort-over-cohort trend showing they hold or improve with scale, not just a healthy snapshot.
  • Pricing should show a deliberate evolution (new tiers, usage-based add-ons) as the company has matured, not a static sheet.
  • Every metric on this slide should be shown as a trend across cohorts or segments, not a single point in time.
