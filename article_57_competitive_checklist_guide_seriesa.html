How to read the Series A Competitive checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Series A, investors aren't asking whether you can beat today's competitors. They're asking whether you can still beat tomorrow's, once your Series C-funded rival notices what you built.

The Series A competitive checklist has four lines, and each pushes the moat conversation forward in time. It's no longer enough for an advantage to exist right now; it needs to compound as the company scales and survive a competitor throwing real money at closing the gap. This guide unpacks all four lines: what each is really verifying, the common way founders half-satisfy it, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Advantage shown to strengthen as you scale, not weaken

This line is checking whether your advantage has a growth mechanism built in, meaning it gets stronger with more customers, more data, or more volume, rather than being a fixed head start that erodes as competitors catch up. Investors want the actual curve, not just an assertion that the moat compounds.

Common miss:
Asserting "our lead widens over time" without showing any metric that has actually strengthened as the company grew, such as accuracy, margin, or retention improving with scale.
What "done" looks like:
Solene Ops, a demand-forecasting platform for regional grocery chains, shows: "Forecast accuracy improved from 81% at 12 stores to 94% at 210 stores, because each new store's sales data retrains the model," says founder Imogen Aslett, with the accuracy curve plotted against store count.
2
Structural defensibility explained beyond current execution

This line is verifying that the moat isn't just "we execute better," which is a claim about the team's current performance rather than a structural barrier a competitor faces. Structural defensibility means something a competitor would have to solve even if they hired an equally good team, such as exclusive data access, integration depth, or contractual lock-in.

Common miss:
Pointing to execution speed or product velocity as the moat, which describes the current team's advantage but not a barrier that persists if execution quality converges.
What "done" looks like:
"We hold exclusive multi-year data-sharing agreements with 3 of the 5 largest regional grocery distributors, giving us POS-level demand signal a new entrant cannot access at any execution speed," Imogen explains, with the agreements referenced in the data room.
3
Differentiation stated at scale, not just today

This line is checking whether the differentiation claim still holds at 10 times the current size, not just at the current customer count. A feature that's differentiated with 50 customers can become table stakes at 500, so the founder needs to project forward and name what still sets the product apart once the market matures.

Common miss:
Restating today's feature advantage without addressing whether it will still be distinct once every serious competitor has had two more years to build a version of it.
What "done" looks like:
"At 210 stores our forecasting is already 13 points more accurate than the closest competitor's published benchmark, and that gap has widened at each 50-store milestone, not narrowed," Imogen states, showing the gap-over-time chart.
4
Risk of a well-funded competitor closing the gap addressed

This line is verifying that you've priced in the realistic worst case: a competitor with significant capital deciding to target your exact position. Addressing it means naming the specific resource, relationship, or dataset that capital alone can't quickly buy, not just asserting confidence.

Common miss:
Dismissing the risk with "we'd see them coming" or "we have too much of a head start," without naming what a well-funded entrant actually couldn't replicate quickly with capital.
What "done" looks like:
"A well-funded entrant could buy engineering talent and match our UI in months, but our distributor data-sharing agreements are exclusive for 4 more years, and grocery chains rarely run parallel forecasting tools during a contract term," Imogen states, naming the specific renewal timeline.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Our lead widens over time" (no metric shown against scale)
  • "We execute faster than anyone else" (not structural, converges if execution does)
  • Today's feature gap restated with no view of it at 10x scale
  • "We'd see a well-funded competitor coming" (no specific barrier named)
Actually done
  • "Forecast accuracy: 81% at 12 stores to 94% at 210 stores, plotted against scale"
  • "Exclusive multi-year data agreements with 3 of the top 5 regional distributors"
  • "13-point accuracy gap, widening at each 50-store milestone, not narrowing"
  • "Distributor exclusivity locked for 4 more years; chains rarely run parallel tools mid-contract"

The pattern across all four: Series A defensibility has to be shown moving in the right direction over time, on a real chart, against real contract terms, not asserted as a permanent state. A moat that isn't plotted against scale is still a guess.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: if a competitor raised 3 times our capital tomorrow, what specifically would still take them years, not months, to replicate? If the honest answer is "our team's speed," the moat isn't structural yet.

Key takeaways
  • Show a real metric strengthening with scale, not just an assertion that it will.
  • Separate structural defensibility (contracts, exclusive data, lock-in) from team execution speed.
  • Project differentiation forward to what it looks like at 10x today's size, not just today.
  • Name the specific barrier a well-funded competitor couldn't buy quickly with capital.
  • Series A moats need to be plotted against time and scale, not asserted as permanent.
