Traction beyond revenue: the signals that count before you have paying customers

SheBloomsTraction

"We don't have revenue yet" is not the same as "we have no traction." Investors at Pre-Seed rarely expect meaningful revenue — they're looking for evidence that real people want what you're building, in whatever form that evidence currently exists.

Revenue is the strongest traction signal, but it's not the only one, and demanding it too early can push founders toward vanity paying customers instead of building the right thing. Here's what else counts, roughly in order of how much it tends to convince an investor.

Paid pilots
A customer pays (even a reduced or symbolic amount) to trial your product for a defined period before committing to a full contract.
Why it counts: money changing hands is the strongest non-revenue signal there is — it proves the problem is worth paying to solve, even at small scale.
Letters of Intent LOI
A signed, non-binding document from a prospective customer stating they intend to buy once the product is ready, often with a specific price and timeline attached.
Why it counts: a named company willing to put something in writing is meaningfully stronger evidence than a verbal "we'd probably use this." Weaker if vague, unsigned, or from a personal contact rather than a real buying decision-maker.
Pre-orders / paid waitlists
Prospective customers pay a deposit or the full price in advance of the product existing, to reserve access or a discount.
Why it counts: money at risk before the product exists is a stronger signal than a free waitlist — it filters out people who were only curious, not genuinely willing to buy.
Waitlists unpaid
People who sign up with just an email to be notified when the product launches, with no payment or commitment attached.
Why it counts: weakest signal on this list, but not worthless — a large, fast-growing waitlist from your actual target customer (not a general audience) shows demand exists, even if it doesn't prove willingness to pay. Always report the conversion rate alongside the count, not the count alone.
Design partnerships
A prospective customer agrees to work closely with you — giving feedback, testing early versions, co-defining requirements — usually in exchange for early or discounted access later.
Why it counts: time invested by a real potential customer is a costlier signal than an opinion — people don't spend hours in feedback sessions for products they don't need.
Distribution partnerships
An agreement with another company to distribute, resell, or integrate your product to their existing customer base, even before your product is fully built.
Why it counts: a partner staking their own customer relationship on your product is a strong external validation signal, and it usually implies a path to real usage once live.

How to report these on a traction slide

Always pair the signal with a number and a filter: how many, what fraction of your actual target customer (not a general audience), and — where possible — how it's trending over time. "40 people on a waitlist" is weak; "40 restaurant owners on a waitlist after posting in three restaurant-owner Facebook groups, growing 15% week over week" is a real signal.

Worked example
A Pre-Seed founder building inventory software for boutique retailers has no paying customers yet, but has: 3 signed LOIs from boutique owners committing to a paid pilot at launch, a 200-person waitlist built entirely from targeted outreach to boutique owner communities (not a general audience), and one active design partnership with a 12-store chain providing weekly feedback. Reported together with specifics, this reads as real, filtered demand — stronger than a vague "we have interest" claim, even with zero revenue.
Non-revenue signals decay fast — convert or lose credibility

LOIs and waitlists are a starting point, not an endpoint. Investors will ask what happened to last quarter's LOIs at your next raise — if they didn't convert to paying pilots, that's a red flag, not a footnote. Track conversion, not just accumulation.

The right mix of signals depends on your business model and price point — a $50k enterprise contract needs LOIs and pilots, while a $10/month consumer app can lean more on waitlist growth and early usage data. Match the evidence to how your actual customers buy.
