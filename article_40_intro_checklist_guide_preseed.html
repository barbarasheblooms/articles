How to read the Pre-Seed Intro checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

The intro slide is on screen for about eight seconds before an investor's attention moves to the next line. At Pre-Seed, that sentence is doing more work than any other single line in the deck, and most founders write it as an afterthought.

The Pre-Seed intro checklist has four lines. Together they test whether the founder can compress a whole company into a sentence, whether that sentence is honest about where the product actually is today, and whether it has survived contact with another human being before it reached an investor. Here's what each line is really checking, where founders quietly fall short, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
One-liner written in a single sentence

This line is testing compression, not eloquence. A founder who can hold the whole company in one sentence has done the hard work of deciding what matters and what doesn't; a founder who needs two or three sentences stitched together with "and" is usually still deciding. The single-sentence constraint forces that decision before the meeting starts.

Common miss:
Writing a one-liner that's technically one sentence but strung together with semicolons or multiple "and" clauses, effectively smuggling in three ideas under the cover of one period.
What "done" looks like:
"Kindred is a shared calendar for divorced co-parents that turns a court custody schedule into automatic pickup reminders, so neither parent has to renegotiate logistics by text."
2
Persona named specifically (not "everyone")

This line checks whether the founder has actually chosen a first user, not just a market. Naming a broad category ("parents," "small businesses") signals the founder hasn't yet decided who they're building for first, which usually means the product hasn't been built for anyone in particular either.

Common miss:
Naming a demographic ("co-parents") instead of a situation ("co-parents with a court-ordered custody schedule who are still coordinating logistics by text"), which is broad enough to include people who don't actually have the problem.
What "done" looks like:
"Our first users are separated parents with a court-ordered custody schedule who currently coordinate pickups and expenses over text with their ex."
3
Reflects what the product actually does today, not just the idea

This line exists because founders naturally describe the vision, not the build. An intro that describes a future feature as if it were live sets an expectation that collapses the moment an investor asks for a demo, and it's one of the fastest ways to lose trust in a first meeting.

Common miss:
Describing a headline feature that's still on the roadmap ("AI-powered dispute resolution for co-parents") when the shipped product is a shared calendar with reminders and nothing resembling AI yet.
What "done" looks like:
"Kindred today is a synced calendar and expense-splitting log for two households. We say that, not 'AI mediation for co-parents,' because the mediation feature doesn't exist yet."
4
Reviewed out loud with a mentor or peer

A sentence can read cleanly on a slide and still fall apart the moment it's spoken. This line checks that the one-liner has been tested against a real listener, someone who can say "wait, who is this for again?" before an investor gets the chance to think it silently.

Common miss:
Only reading the line silently, or having a co-founder who already knows the business proofread it, which doesn't test whether a stranger can actually follow it.
What "done" looks like:
"Said the one-liner to a friend outside the industry twice. The second time, she repeated back the persona and the problem accurately without being prompted."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Kindred helps busy parents manage their lives together." (two ideas stitched together, no clear single claim)
  • "Built for parents everywhere." (a demographic, not a situation with a problem attached)
  • "We're building an AI co-parenting assistant." (describes the roadmap, not the shipped product)
  • Never said the line out loud to anyone before the deck went out.
Actually done
  • "Kindred syncs custody schedules for separated parents so they stop renegotiating pickups by text."
  • "Built for separated parents with a court-ordered custody schedule."
  • "Today, Kindred is a shared calendar and expense log; AI features are on the roadmap, not live."
  • Said the line to three people outside the company and refined it after each conversation.

The pattern across all four: the intro slide rewards restraint. Every extra clause, every unnamed persona, every feature borrowed from the roadmap dilutes the one thing an investor needs to walk away remembering. Say less, but make sure what you say is exactly true today.

A useful test

Read your one-liner out loud to someone who has never heard of your company. If they can't repeat back the persona and the problem in their own words within a few seconds, the sentence is either too dense, too vague, or describing something that isn't built yet. Fix that before you fix anything else on the slide.

For the underlying structure a strong opening line needs, see "The Greatest Sales Deck Ever Made" by Andy Raskin, or work through the Value Proposition Canvas to pressure-test whether your persona and problem actually line up.

Key takeaways
  • The one-liner should be a single, undiluted claim, not two or three ideas joined by "and."
  • Name a situation, not a demographic. "Parents" is a category; "separated parents with a court-ordered schedule" is a persona.
  • Never describe a roadmap feature as if it's live. The gap surfaces the moment someone asks for a demo.
  • Testing the line out loud catches confusion that reading it silently never will.
  • Specificity and honesty about the current build matter more than sounding impressive.
