How to read the Series A Financials checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Series A, the financials slide stops being about whether the company can survive and starts being about whether it's an efficient machine worth pouring more fuel into.

The Series A financials checklist has four lines. Each one assumes a level of financial fluency the earlier rounds didn't require: efficiency ratios, cost structure by category, a real answer on the endgame. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
18-36 month projections with sensitivity analysis included

This line is checking whether the founder understands which variables actually move the business, not just whether a long-range projection exists. Sensitivity analysis means showing how the model responds when one key input (churn, sales cycle length, average contract value) shifts, so the reader can see which assumptions the plan is most exposed to.

Common miss:
Extending the base-case model out to 36 months without testing how the plan holds up if churn or sales cycle assumptions come in worse than expected.
What "done" looks like:
Renata Souza, founder of Ledgerline, a compliance automation platform for mid-market logistics firms, shows: "Base case assumes 1.8% monthly churn. At 3% churn, ARR at month 30 falls from $9.4M to $7.1M, still funding the plan but pushing Series B timing back one quarter."
2
Magic Number and Burn Multiple stated

This line is verifying that the founder measures growth efficiency using the same vocabulary Series A investors use to compare companies across a portfolio, not just revenue growth in isolation. Magic Number and Burn Multiple translate "we're growing" into "we're growing efficiently relative to what we spend," which is the actual question at this stage.

Common miss:
Reporting revenue growth rate and burn separately without combining them into the ratios investors will calculate anyway, leaving the efficiency story untold.
What "done" looks like:
"Magic Number of 0.9 (net new ARR of $1.35M against $1.5M in prior-quarter sales and marketing spend), Burn Multiple of 1.4 (net burn of $1.89M against $1.35M net new ARR), both trending toward benchmark as sales cycle shortens."
3
Runway and monthly burn broken down by category (salaries vs. other opex)

This line is checking whether the founder can explain what the money is actually being spent on, not just the total burn figure. A single burn number hides whether the company is capital-efficient (mostly salaries, low fixed overhead) or carrying costs that don't scale, and Series A investors want to see that distinction made explicit.

Common miss:
Reporting a single monthly burn figure with runway in months, with no split showing how much is salaries versus infrastructure, tooling, and other operating costs.
What "done" looks like:
"$620K monthly burn: $460K salaries (32 FTEs), $95K cloud infrastructure and compliance tooling, $65K other opex. 22 months of runway at current burn."
4
Exit strategy and expected breakeven or next-round timing addressed

This line is verifying that the founder has a point of view on how this capital eventually returns money to investors, whether through profitability, acquisition, or a clearly timed next raise. It's not asking for a firm commitment, it's asking whether the founder has thought about the endgame at all rather than treating each round as its own isolated event.

Common miss:
Skipping the topic entirely, or deflecting with "we're focused on growth right now" without naming a next-round timeline or a plausible acquirer category.
What "done" looks like:
"Targeting Series B readiness at $12M ARR in Q3 2028; strategic acquirers in the compliance and logistics-software space (three have made comparable acquisitions in the past 24 months) are a secondary path if growth outpaces our ability to scale go-to-market alone."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • 36-month model with no test of what happens if churn or sales cycle worsens
  • "Revenue growing 60% YoY" (no Magic Number or Burn Multiple stated)
  • "$620K monthly burn, 22 months of runway" (no category breakdown)
  • "We're focused on growth right now" (no next-round or exit timing)
Actually done
  • "At 3% churn, ARR falls from $9.4M to $7.1M, Series B pushed one quarter"
  • "Magic Number 0.9, Burn Multiple 1.4, both trending toward benchmark"
  • "$460K salaries, $95K infrastructure, $65K other, 22 months runway"
  • "Series B readiness at $12M ARR in Q3 2028; three plausible acquirers"

The pattern across all four: at Series A, the checklist is measuring fluency in the language investors already use to compare companies. Restating your own numbers in that language, rather than making the investor translate for you, is most of what separates a checked box from a genuinely done one.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: have I expressed this the way an investor's own model would express it? A sensitivity range instead of a single line, a Magic Number and Burn Multiple instead of a growth percentage, a cost breakdown instead of a total, and a named next-round or exit path instead of silence.

Key takeaways
  • An 18-36 month projection needs a sensitivity range showing what happens to key outcomes when a core assumption worsens.
  • Magic Number and Burn Multiple translate growth into growth efficiency, the actual question at Series A.
  • Burn should be broken down by category (salaries vs. other opex) so investors can see how capital-efficient the cost structure really is.
  • Exit strategy or next-round timing needs a named path and date, not a deflection toward "focusing on growth."
  • Across all four lines, restating your numbers in the vocabulary investors already use beats making them do the translation themselves.
