How to run a competitor analysis, step by step

SheBloomsCompetitive landscape

"We don't really have competitors" is the fastest way to signal to an investor that you haven't looked. Every business competes with something — even if it's a spreadsheet, a manual process, or doing nothing at all.

A competitor analysis is not a one-time slide you fill in before a pitch — it's a repeatable process for understanding who else is solving your customer's problem, how, and why a customer would pick one option over another. Here's the process, in order.

1
List direct competitors
Companies solving the same problem, for the same customer, in a similar way. Find them through: search terms your buyer would use ("best [category] for [use case]"), review sites (G2, Capterra, app store categories), funding databases (Crunchbase, PitchBook) for your category, and simply asking your first 10-20 customers what they considered before you.
2
List indirect competitors and alternatives
The status quo is always a competitor: spreadsheets, manual processes, a generalist tool being used as a workaround, an agency, or simply "not solving this problem right now." Indirect competitors matter because they set the bar your solution has to beat on convenience and price, even when no other dedicated product exists.
3
Gather real information on each one, not impressions
Pricing pages, product demos, review sites (read the 3-star reviews — they show real gaps, not marketing copy), G2/Capterra comparison pages, and their own marketing to see how they position themselves. Where possible, talk to a few of their customers or ex-customers directly.
4
Choose a comparison framework
A feature matrix, a 2x2 positioning map, or a Porter's Five Forces view each surface different things — see the companion article on frameworks to pick the one that matches what you're trying to learn (see the Frameworks article and the Positioning Map tool).
5
Identify where you actually differ, and why it's hard to copy
List every difference you find, then filter ruthlessly: does this difference matter to the customer's buying decision, and could a funded competitor copy it in a quarter? What survives that filter is your real differentiation — see the companion article on defensible differentiation.
6
Revisit it every round, not just once
The competitive landscape shifts constantly, and what counted as differentiation at Pre-Seed (founder insight, early product bets) is not what counts at Series A (structural moats that get stronger with scale). Re-run this process each time you raise, and whenever a new competitor enters your search results.
Worked example
A founder building a scheduling tool for hair salons lists three direct competitors (dedicated salon software) and two indirect ones (Google Calendar + a paper booking book, which is what most of her target customers actually use today). Reading 3-star reviews of the direct competitors, she finds a recurring complaint: none of them handle walk-in clients well. She validates this gap with 15 salon owner interviews, confirms it's a real, frequent pain point, and builds her differentiation case around walk-in handling — a specific, evidenced gap, not a vague "better product" claim.
A competitor analysis is evidence, not decoration

Investors aren't grading whether you found every logo to put in a grid. They're checking whether your understanding of the landscape actually informs your product and go-to-market decisions. If your competitor analysis doesn't change anything about your roadmap or positioning, it wasn't a real analysis.

This process is a starting framework — adapt the depth to your stage. A Pre-Seed analysis can run on desk research and customer conversations; a Series A analysis should include harder evidence like win/loss data and churn-to-competitor tracking.
