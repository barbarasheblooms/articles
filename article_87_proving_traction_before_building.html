How to prove traction before you've built the product: smoke tests, concierge MVPs, and fake-door tests

SheBloomsTraction

You don't need a finished product to know whether people want it. These four tests let you gather real evidence of demand in days or weeks, before you spend months building something nobody buys.

1
Fake-door test (also called a "smoke test")
Publish a landing page, ad, or button describing the product as if it exists, and measure how many people click through to "buy" or "sign up" before hitting a "coming soon, join the waitlist" message. Measures: click-through rate, email capture rate, and — if you show a real price — how many proceed past that point. Cheapest and fastest test on this list; weakest signal because no money or real commitment changes hands.
2
Concierge MVP
Deliver the outcome manually, using humans and spreadsheets instead of software, to a small number of real customers at your real (or close to real) target price. Measures: whether people pay for the outcome, how they react to using it, and where the manual process breaks down — which becomes your first product spec. Slower and more effort than a fake-door test, but produces far stronger evidence, including real payment and real usage feedback.
3
Wizard of Oz test
Build a real front-end interface that customers interact with, but power it manually behind the scenes instead of with the automated system you'll eventually build. Measures: how customers actually behave with a product-like interface, without committing engineering time to the automation yet. Useful when the interface itself, not just the outcome, needs testing (e.g. a matching algorithm you're initially running by hand).
4
Paid pre-sale / pilot
Ask real prospective customers to pay — even a deposit, even a reduced price — for something that doesn't fully exist yet, with a clear delivery date. Measures: true willingness to pay, which is the single strongest signal any of these tests can produce. Requires enough credibility (a demo, a clear spec, a trusted intro) that a prospect is willing to commit money to something unbuilt.

Choosing the right test for your stage

Start cheap and fast, then escalate as each test gives you a "yes, keep going" signal. A fake-door test that gets no clicks tells you to rethink the concept before spending a single hour on a concierge test. A concierge MVP that converts to a handful of paid pilots tells you the manual process is worth automating.

Worked example
A founder wants to build a service that matches freelance nurses with short-notice shifts at clinics. Before writing code, she runs a fake-door test (a simple landing page describing the service) that gets a 4% signup rate from clinic administrators she reached via LinkedIn — encouraging but not conclusive. She then runs a two-week concierge MVP: manually matching 8 real shifts using a spreadsheet and WhatsApp, charging clinics a placement fee. Five of eight matches succeed, three clinics ask to continue past the trial, and two pay the full fee without prompting. That's real traction — a working, tested outcome and real payment — before a single line of matching-algorithm code exists.
A test that shows no interest is a good outcome, not a failed one

The point of these tests is to find out cheaply whether an idea works, including finding out that it doesn't. A fake-door test with a 0.3% click rate after real traffic just saved you six months of building the wrong thing — that's a successful test, even though the underlying idea needs rethinking.

These tests produce real signal but not a scaled business — investors will still expect a credible plan for how the manual or partial process becomes a real, scalable product. Use these tests to justify that plan with evidence, not to replace it.
