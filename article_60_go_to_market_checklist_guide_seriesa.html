How to read the Series A Go-to-market checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Series A, the go-to-market checklist stops asking whether growth works and starts asking whether growth works without the founder in the room. It wants a playbook a team can run, with the efficiency math to prove it deserves more capital.

The Series A go-to-market checklist has four lines. Each is checking a different piece of the same question: can this motion survive being handed to other people and being scaled with real dollars. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
GTM playbook documented and repeatable across a team, not founder-led

This line is checking whether sales and marketing success depends on a written process a new hire could follow, or whether it depends on the founder's personal relationships and instincts. A real playbook names the stages, the scripts, the qualification criteria, and the tools, in enough detail that someone hired next month could run it.

Common miss:
Pointing to strong revenue growth as proof of a playbook, when in practice the founder is still closing every meaningful deal personally and no one else has replicated the process.
What "done" looks like:
Briar Health (clinic scheduling software) has a written 5-stage sales playbook covering qualification criteria, a demo script, objection handling for its top 6 objections, and CRM stage definitions; 4 of its 6 account executives, hired in the last 9 months, are now closing at within 15% of the two tenured reps' win rate.
2
Magic Number and sales efficiency stated

This is checking whether you track the standard efficiency metric investors use to compare sales motions across companies, and whether you understand what's driving it, not just whether you can quote a number. The Magic Number (net new ARR in a quarter divided by sales and marketing spend from the prior quarter) tells investors how much a dollar of go-to-market spend is currently worth.

Common miss:
Quoting a Magic Number without stating the underlying inputs (new ARR, prior-quarter S&M spend) or without explaining a recent dip or rise in the trend.
What "done" looks like:
Briar Health's Magic Number is 0.92 this quarter (up from 0.71 two quarters ago), driven by the new playbook lifting AE ramp time from 5 months to 3, calculated as $1.38M net new ARR divided by $1.5M prior-quarter S&M spend.
3
Strategic partnerships supporting distribution included

This line is checking whether you have a second distribution leg beyond direct sales, one that lowers CAC or extends reach in a way direct effort alone can't. It's verifying the partnership actually moves pipeline or revenue, not that a logo sits on a partnerships slide.

Common miss:
Listing signed partnership agreements or MOUs as evidence of distribution, when none have yet produced a qualified lead or a closed deal.
What "done" looks like:
Briar Health's integration and co-marketing partnership with a regional EHR vendor has produced 34 qualified leads and 9 closed deals in 5 months, at a blended CAC 40% lower than direct outbound, because the EHR vendor's sales team pre-qualifies fit before the introduction.
4
Payback period and CAC shown at scale

This is checking whether your unit economics still hold as spend has grown, not just in the small, favorable sample from early traction. CAC and payback often drift as a company moves past its warmest audience and into a broader market, and investors want to see that drift accounted for honestly.

Common miss:
Reusing an early-stage CAC and payback figure from a much smaller cohort, without disclosing how those numbers have moved as spend and headcount scaled.
What "done" looks like:
Briar Health's blended CAC has risen from $4,200 at 20 customers to $6,800 at 140 customers as outbound moved past its warmest segment, with payback holding at 11 months against a $61k average contract value, and a stated plan to bring CAC back toward $5,500 as the partnership channel takes a larger share of new logos.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Revenue is growing fast" (the founder is still closing every meaningful deal personally)
  • "Our Magic Number is strong" (no stated inputs, no explanation of the trend behind it)
  • "We have three signed partnerships" (none have produced a lead or a closed deal yet)
  • "CAC and payback remain healthy" (reused early-stage figures that ignore how spend has scaled)
Actually done
  • "4 of 6 AEs hired in 9 months close within 15% of our tenured reps' win rate" (playbook works without the founder)
  • "Magic Number 0.92, up from 0.71, driven by AE ramp time dropping from 5 to 3 months" (inputs and driver stated)
  • "Our EHR partnership produced 34 qualified leads and 9 closed deals at 40% lower CAC" (measurable pipeline impact)
  • "CAC rose from $4,200 to $6,800 as we scaled past our warmest segment; payback holds at 11 months" (honest drift, disclosed)

The pattern across all four: at Series A, investors are testing whether the growth engine is an asset that scales with capital, or a founder-dependent motion that will plateau the moment the founder's attention is split across other things.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: would this still be true if the founder took a two-week vacation, and does the number reflect current scale or an early, flattering sample? If the answer depends on the founder's personal effort or an outdated cohort, the line isn't done yet.

Key takeaways
  • A real playbook is proven by new hires performing close to tenured reps, not by revenue growth alone.
  • Magic Number needs its inputs stated and its trend explained, not just quoted as a single figure.
  • Partnerships count as distribution once they've produced leads or closed deals, not when they're signed.
  • CAC and payback should be reported at current scale, with honest disclosure of how they've drifted.
  • Investors are testing whether growth survives the founder stepping back, not whether it's currently fast.
