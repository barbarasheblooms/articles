How to read the Pre-Seed Closing checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

The closing slide is the one founders rush most, because by then they've already said the important things. It's also the slide investors remember on the drive home.

The Pre-Seed closing checklist has four lines. Each one asks whether the ask itself, and the note you leave on, is as specific and considered as the rest of the deck. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Raise amount and instrument stated (SAFE, equity)

This line is checking whether the founder can state the mechanics of the ask in one breath, not just the headline number. The instrument matters as much as the amount, since a SAFE, a priced round, and a convertible note each carry different implications for dilution and future negotiation, and investors want to know which conversation they're actually walking into.

Common miss:
Stating only the amount ("raising $300K") and leaving the instrument, cap, and discount to be asked about, rather than volunteered upfront.
What "done" looks like:
Talia Reyes, founder of Pantryloop, a shelf-life tracking app for small grocers, closes with: "We're raising $300K on a post-money SAFE, $4.5M cap, no discount."
2
Milestones this capital unlocks listed, tied to the next round

This line is verifying that the founder has connected the money to specific outcomes, not just runway. Investors are not funding survival for its own sake, they're funding proof points that make the next round easier to raise. Listing the milestones this money buys, and explicitly tying them to what a Seed investor will want to see, shows the founder is already thinking one round ahead.

Common miss:
Listing generic milestones like "grow the team" or "expand marketing" that describe activity rather than a proof point a future investor would recognize.
What "done" looks like:
"This $300K gets us to 40 paying grocers and $9K MRR by month 14, the threshold our target seed investors have told us they want to see before leading a round."
3
Current commitments, if any, noted

This line is checking whether the founder is transparent about how much of the round is already spoken for, which changes how an investor reads their own urgency to act. Stating commitments, even informal soft-circles, signals momentum and also sets honest expectations about how much room is actually left in the round.

Common miss:
Staying silent on commitments entirely, which reads as either having none (undercutting momentum) or hiding a nearly-closed round (creating false urgency once discovered).
What "done" looks like:
"$110K of the $300K is soft-circled: $60K from a pre-seed fund that led our first conversation, $50K from two angel operators in grocery retail."
4
Vision sentence written to close strong

This line is verifying that the last thing an investor hears is memorable and specific to this company, not a generic statement about changing an industry. A strong closing vision sentence connects the near-term milestones back to the larger reason the company exists, in language distinct enough that someone could recall it a week later without notes.

Common miss:
Closing with an industry-wide platitude ("we're going to transform how small businesses manage inventory") that could belong to almost any company in the space.
What "done" looks like:
"Every year, small grocers throw away 8% of shelf inventory they never needed to lose. Pantryloop exists to make that number zero, one shelf at a time."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Raising $300K" (instrument, cap, and discount left unstated)
  • "This will help us grow the team" (activity, not a milestone)
  • No mention of commitments (unclear if none exist or it's nearly closed)
  • "We're going to transform how small businesses manage inventory"
Actually done
  • "$300K on a post-money SAFE, $4.5M cap, no discount"
  • "Gets us to 40 paying grocers, $9K MRR, by month 14"
  • "$110K of $300K soft-circled, from a fund and two angels"
  • "Grocers waste 8% of shelf inventory. We exist to make that zero."

The pattern across all four: the closing slide rewards founders who treat the ask itself as something worth being precise about, right down to the sentence they leave hanging in the room.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: if the investor repeated this back to a partner from memory tomorrow, would it still be accurate and specific to us? The instrument and terms, the milestone and its number, the commitment status, and the closing line should all survive being retold secondhand.

Key takeaways
  • State the raise amount and the instrument, including cap and discount, together rather than leaving terms to be asked about.
  • Milestones should be proof points a future investor would recognize, not descriptions of internal activity.
  • Disclose current commitments, even informal soft-circles, so investors can read momentum and remaining room honestly.
  • The closing vision sentence should be specific enough to this company that it couldn't be mistaken for a competitor's line.
  • Across all four lines, precision in the ask and the close matters as much as precision earlier in the deck.
