How to read the Seed problem checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 6 min read

A checklist item is a compressed question. Ticking the box without unpacking what's behind it is the fastest way to walk into a Seed meeting with a slide that looks finished but isn't.

The Seed problem checklist has four lines. Each one reads as a short instruction, but each is really shorthand for a specific thing an investor is trying to verify. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is actually asking, the most common way founders satisfy the letter of it without satisfying the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Problem quantified with your own customer data, not just interviews

At Pre-Seed, interview quotes were enough. At Seed, the checklist is asking you to move from "people told us this hurts" to "here is a number, sourced from our own paying customers, that says how much." The word doing the work here is own: a market report or a competitor's public numbers do not satisfy this line, no matter how relevant they are.

Common miss:
Restating the Pre-Seed interview findings with slightly more confident language, without any new number pulled from actual usage or onboarding data since customers started paying.
What "done" looks like:
A specific figure (hours, euros, error rate, missed revenue) with a stated source: "onboarding survey, N=52 of 80 customers" or "in-product usage logs, last 90 days."
2
Cost to customers stated as a concrete number

This line is checking that the quantification from line one is actually usable in conversation. A number buried in a footnote or expressed only as a range ("a few hours a week, roughly") does not read as concrete. Investors want a figure they can repeat back to you, and eventually to their partners.

Common miss:
Quantifying the problem once, deep in the appendix or the write-up, but never surfacing a single headline number on the actual slide.
What "done" looks like:
One sentence that could stand alone: "This costs our average customer €X per month" or "Our customers lose N hours a week to this before adopting us." If you can't finish that sentence today, this line isn't done yet, regardless of what the interviews said.
3
Paying customers confirm the thesis in practice

This is the line most often confused with line one. Quantifying the cost is a data exercise; confirming the thesis "in practice" means showing that customers are behaving in a way that proves the problem was real and worth paying to solve, not just describing it accurately when asked.

Common miss:
Treating a satisfaction survey score as proof, when what actually confirms the thesis is behavioral: renewal, usage frequency, upgrade rate, or customers voluntarily citing the problem as their reason for buying.
What "done" looks like:
A behavioral signal tied explicitly to the problem: "71% of customers who cited [the problem] in onboarding have since renewed or upgraded" or "customers who report the highest cost from this problem also show the highest usage frequency."
4
Why this problem is still relevant and urgent explained

This carries over from Pre-Seed, but the bar moves. At Pre-Seed, urgency could rest on interview language ("she said she needed this yesterday"). At Seed, the checklist wants urgency grounded in a trend, ideally the same data you used to quantify the cost: is the cost per customer flat, growing, or shrinking since you started measuring it?

Common miss:
Reusing the Pre-Seed urgency framing word-for-word, with no reference to how the problem has moved (or hasn't) since customers started paying.
What "done" looks like:
A one-line trend statement: "cold-outreach conversion for our segment fell from 2.1% to 1.6% over the last 12 months," directly tied to the same metric used elsewhere on the slide.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Customers told us this is a real problem" (still interview-based)
  • "It costs them a meaningful amount of time" (no number)
  • "Customers are happy with the product" (satisfaction, not behavior)
  • Urgency language copied from the Pre-Seed slide, unchanged
Actually done
  • "Onboarding data (N=52 of 80) shows customers spent 4.8 hrs/month on this before us"
  • "That time is worth roughly €280/month at their own billable rate"
  • "71% who cited this problem at onboarding have since upgraded"
  • "Cost per customer is not falling: conversion dropped from 2.1% to 1.6% in 12 months"

Notice the pattern: every item on the right names a source, a number, and where relevant, a comparison over time. That triad, source, number, trend, is what an investor is really scanning for behind each checklist line, whether or not the checklist says so explicitly.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, try to finish this sentence: "We know this because [source], and the number is [figure]." If you can complete all four sentences without hedging, the checklist item is genuinely done. If you're reaching for adjectives instead of numbers, it isn't yet, even if the box is ticked in the app.

Key takeaways
  • Each checklist line is a compressed question; unpack what it's actually verifying before deciding it's done.
  • "Own customer data" excludes market reports and competitor numbers, however relevant, it must come from your paying base.
  • A concrete cost figure needs to work as a standalone sentence, not sit buried in an appendix.
  • "Confirms the thesis in practice" means behavioral evidence (renewal, usage, upgrades), not satisfaction scores.
  • Urgency at Seed needs a trend line, not a repeat of the Pre-Seed interview quote.
