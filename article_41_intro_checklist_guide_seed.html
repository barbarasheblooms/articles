How to read the Seed Intro checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

By Seed, the intro slide isn't introducing an idea anymore, it's introducing a business that already has evidence behind it. The checklist changes accordingly: it stops asking whether you can describe the company and starts asking whether you can prove it.

The Seed intro checklist has four lines. Two of them are new compared to Pre-Seed, built specifically around the fact that there should now be real numbers to lead with. Here's what each line actually verifies, the common way founders undersell their own traction, and what a complete answer looks like.

1
Opens with a real growth metric (users, ARR, or MoM), not a mission statement

This line is checking whether the founder leads with evidence or with belief. A mission statement is unfalsifiable, anyone can say they believe in their customers; a growth metric is a claim an investor can actually evaluate. At Seed, leading with mission first signals that the traction, if it exists, isn't strong enough to open with.

Common miss:
Opening with "We believe every agency deserves better financial tools" and burying the actual MRR figure two slides later, as if the mission is more persuasive than the number.
What "done" looks like:
"Fernbank opened with: '$41,000 MRR, growing 19% month over month for the last four months.' The mission statement came after, as context, not headline."
2
Headline number you want investors to remember is explicit

This line checks whether the founder has decided, on purpose, which single number matters most. Investors sit through a lot of pitches; if a slide shows six metrics with equal visual weight, the investor picks whichever one they happen to notice, not the one that actually makes the strongest case.

Common miss:
Displaying MRR, churn, NPS, logo count, CAC, and LTV all on the same slide with the same font size, leaving the investor to guess which one is supposed to stick.
What "done" looks like:
"If you remember one number from this call, remember 19%. That's our month-over-month growth rate for the last four months."
3
Persona named specifically

At Seed, this line checks whether the persona has sharpened along with the traction. A founder now has real usage data showing exactly who's paying and why; carrying forward the same broad persona from the Pre-Seed deck usually means that data hasn't been looked at closely.

Common miss:
Leaving "small business owners" unchanged from the earliest version of the deck, even though 94% of current revenue actually comes from one narrow segment.
What "done" looks like:
"Our paying customers are 2-8 person marketing agencies billing multiple clients monthly, that's 94% of our current revenue, not small businesses broadly."
4
Reviewed out loud with a mentor or peer

This line checks whether the founder can say the headline number out loud, confidently and exactly, without checking notes or rounding it differently than what's written on the slide. A number that gets hedged in conversation undermines the confidence the slide is trying to establish.

Common miss:
Being able to point to the number on the slide but saying "somewhere around 15 to 20 percent, I think" when asked to state it out loud unprompted.
What "done" looks like:
"Practiced the growth number out loud with two advisors; both times, said '19% month over month for four straight months' verbatim without checking notes."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "We believe every agency deserves better financial tools." (mission statement leads, the number is buried)
  • Six metrics shown with equal weight, no indication of which one matters most.
  • "Small business owners" carried over from the Pre-Seed deck unchanged.
  • Hedges on the growth number when asked to say it out loud live.
Actually done
  • "$41,000 MRR, growing 19% MoM for four months." (leads with proof, not belief)
  • "Remember one number: 19% MoM growth." (one number, deliberately chosen)
  • "2-8 person marketing agencies billing multiple clients monthly." (sharpened by real usage data)
  • Recites the growth number verbatim, unprompted, in front of two advisors.

The pattern across all four: proof outranks positioning. At Seed, an investor is looking for the moment the founder stops selling a vision and starts reporting a result, and the intro slide is the first place that shift needs to show up.

A useful test

Cover the bottom half of your intro slide and ask: does the top line contain a number an investor could fact-check, and could I say that number out loud right now without looking? If either answer is no, the slide is still leading with belief instead of evidence.

For the underlying structure a strong opening line needs, see "The Greatest Sales Deck Ever Made" by Andy Raskin, or work through the Value Proposition Canvas to check that your sharpened persona still lines up with the problem you're solving.

Key takeaways
  • Lead with a real growth metric, not a mission statement. The mission can follow as context.
  • Choose one headline number on purpose. Don't make the investor pick from a crowded slide.
  • Sharpen the persona using real usage data instead of carrying forward a broad guess from Pre-Seed.
  • Say the headline number out loud, exactly, before the meeting, not for the first time in it.
  • The Seed intro's job is to show proof has replaced belief as the basis for the pitch.
