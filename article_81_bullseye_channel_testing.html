The Bullseye framework: how to systematically test channels instead of guessing

SheBloomsGo-to-market

Testing five channels a little each is the most common way founders waste a quarter. The Bullseye framework, from Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares' book Traction, exists to stop that.

The core idea: out of roughly nineteen possible marketing and distribution channels, only a handful will ever work well for your specific business — and you almost never know which ones in advance. The framework is a disciplined way to find out fast, cheaply, and without spreading your limited time so thin that nothing gets a real test.

The five steps

1
Brainstorm
List every plausible channel, even ones that seem unlikely — paid search, SEO/content, social, email, PR, events, partnerships, sales, community, affiliate, and so on. The point of this step is coverage, not judgment; you'll cut the list in the next step.
2
Rank
For each channel, estimate roughly: cost to test, likely customer volume, and how well it fits your price point, sales cycle, and buyer type (see the channel-fit framework for that last part). Be honest about which ones you're ranking highly just because they sound exciting rather than because they actually fit.
3
Prioritize
Pick the top 3 candidates from your ranking — not one, not ten. Three is enough to hedge against being wrong about any single channel, and few enough that each gets a real, well-resourced test instead of a token effort.
4
Test
Run small, cheap, time-boxed tests on all 3 in parallel — enough spend and time to get a real read on CAC and volume, but not so much that a bad bet is expensive. A test should answer one question clearly: can this channel produce customers at a cost and volume that could matter to this business.
5
Focus
Take whichever channel (usually just one) showed the strongest signal, and go all-in: more budget, more time, more of your team's attention. Stop the others — don't keep half-running channels that didn't show promise just because you already invested in the test.

Why this beats "just try everything a bit"

Splitting a limited budget and even more limited founder time across five or six channels means none of them gets enough investment to produce a signal you can trust. A channel that might have worked brilliantly with real focus looks like a failure when it only got 15% of your attention. The Bullseye framework forces the discipline of a real test on a short list, rather than a shallow test on a long one.

Worked example
A Pre-Seed B2B tool brainstorms 12 plausible channels, ranks them against price point (mid, $150/mo) and sales cycle (2-3 weeks, one decision-maker), and narrows to 3: LinkedIn outbound, content/SEO targeting a specific search term their buyers use, and a partnership with an adjacent tool used by the same audience. They spend a focused two weeks on each: outbound produces 4 customers at a €180 CAC, content produces early traffic but zero customers yet (too early to judge), and the partnership produces 6 warm intros and 2 customers with a much lower effective CAC. They focus their next quarter on the partnership and scale it, while letting content keep compounding in the background at low cost, and pausing outbound.
A test isn't a failure just because it didn't win

The goal of the test step is information, not validation of your favorite channel. A channel that clearly doesn't work is just as valuable a result as one that does — it tells you where not to spend the next quarter, which is worth more than the cost of the test.

This framework is a prioritization tool, not a guarantee — some businesses genuinely need more than 3 tests before finding a working channel, especially in narrow or emerging markets. Use it to stay disciplined, not to force a result on an artificial timeline.
