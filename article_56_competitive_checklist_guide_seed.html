How to read the Seed Competitive checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Seed, "we move faster" stops being a moat. The checklist wants to know what actually stops a competitor from catching up once they notice you.

The Seed competitive checklist has four lines, and all four push past differentiation into defensibility. A feature lead is real, but it's temporary; investors at this stage want evidence that something structural protects the business once the market notices it's working. This guide unpacks all four lines: what each is really verifying, the common way founders half-satisfy it, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Defensible moat named with evidence (IP, network effects, data, switching costs)

This line is checking whether you can name the specific category of moat you're building, backed by evidence, rather than describing your product's current strengths. A moat claim needs to fit a recognizable category (IP, network effects, proprietary data, switching costs, or similar) and be supported by something measurable, not just asserted.

Common miss:
Claiming "we have a strong brand" or "our team is our moat," which describes an asset but not a mechanism that gets harder to copy as the company grows.
What "done" looks like:
Kestrel Health, a care coordination platform for home health agencies, states: "Switching costs: agencies that onboard patient care plans into Kestrel take an average of 11 weeks to migrate off, per our own churn-attempt data from 6 agencies that tried," says founder Yara Mensah.
2
Not just "better UX": specific advantage backed by data

This line is verifying that your differentiation claim survives past "it feels nicer to use." UX quality is real but hard to defend and easy for a well-funded competitor to match; investors want a claim that can be measured, like a time saved, an error rate reduced, or a task completed that the alternative can't.

Common miss:
Describing the product as "more intuitive" or "cleaner" than incumbents, with screenshots as the only evidence.
What "done" looks like:
"Care coordinators using Kestrel close a patient intake in an average of 14 minutes, versus 47 minutes on the incumbent tool, measured across 40 coordinators in a side-by-side pilot," Yara reports.
3
What happens if a competitor copies your core feature addressed

This line is checking whether you've actually war-gamed the obvious response: a well-resourced competitor rebuilding your headline feature. Addressing it means naming what would still hold even after the feature gap closes, not insisting the feature can't be copied.

Common miss:
Avoiding the question entirely, or answering with "they'd take too long to catch up," with no explanation of why the timeline matters or what happens if it's wrong.
What "done" looks like:
"If a competitor rebuilds the intake flow, they still lack our 6-agency dataset on which care plans correlate with fewer readmissions, which is what our recommendations engine is actually built on," Yara explains.
4
Evidence the advantage is holding up in the market

This line is verifying the moat isn't just theoretical: it's checking for a real-world signal that the advantage has already survived contact with the market, such as a win against a specific competitor, a retained customer who evaluated an alternative, or a pricing power indicator.

Common miss:
Asserting the moat is durable in the future tense ("this will be hard to copy") with no example yet of it actually holding up against a real competitive situation.
What "done" looks like:
"Two of our agencies evaluated a competing tool during renewal talks this year and stayed with Kestrel, citing the migration cost and existing care plan data," Yara states, naming both agencies with permission.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Our brand and team are our moat" (asset, not a defensibility mechanism)
  • "More intuitive than incumbents" (no measured advantage)
  • "They'd take too long to catch up" (no data-based reasoning)
  • "This will be hard to copy" (future tense, no real-world test yet)
Actually done
  • "11-week average migration time, based on 6 agencies that tried to leave"
  • "14 minutes vs. 47 minutes per intake, measured across 40 coordinators"
  • "Competitor lacks our 6-agency readmission dataset even if they copy the flow"
  • "2 agencies stayed through renewal after evaluating a competitor, named with permission"

The pattern across all four: a moat claim only counts once it's been tested against something real, whether that's a churn attempt, a side-by-side comparison, or a renewal conversation. Anything still in the future tense is a hypothesis, not evidence.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: has this advantage already been tested by a real customer, competitor, or renewal decision, or am I describing what I expect will happen? Seed investors are listening for the difference.

Key takeaways
  • Name your moat as a recognizable category (IP, network effects, data, switching costs) with evidence attached.
  • Replace "better UX" claims with a measured advantage: time, error rate, or completion.
  • Address directly what still holds if a competitor copies your core feature.
  • Bring a real-world example of the advantage surviving contact with a competitor or renewal.
  • A moat only counts once it has been tested; a future-tense claim is still a hypothesis.
