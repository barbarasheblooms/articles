10 financial ratios that tell you if your startup is actually healthy

By SheBlooms 10 min read

Growth tells you the business is getting bigger. It doesn't tell you whether it's getting healthier. These ten ratios are how you check the second thing, before an investor, or a bank, checks it for you.

Most early-stage founders track one or two numbers obsessively, usually MRR and burn, and treat the rest of the balance sheet as something to worry about later. That's reasonable at the very earliest stage, when there isn't much of a balance sheet to speak of yet. But as soon as you have real revenue, real receivables, and real short-term obligations, these ten ratios become the language investors, lenders, and your own board will use to judge whether the business underneath the growth curve is actually sound.

You don't need all ten to be strong on day one. What you need is to know where you stand on each of them, and to be able to explain honestly what you're doing about the ones that aren't yet where they should be. That, more than any single number, is what reads as financial maturity.

Liquidity: can you cover what you owe right now

1. Working Capital
Am I financially balanced?
Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities
The cushion of short-term assets left over once you've covered your short-term liabilities.
Why it matters: a positive number means you can comfortably meet what's due within the year. A negative one is an early warning sign of cash strain, even if the company looks profitable on paper.
Healthy direction: positive, and growing roughly in step with revenue. A working capital figure that's shrinking while revenue grows is worth investigating before it becomes a crisis.
7. Quick Ratio
Do I have liquidity problems?
Quick Ratio = Realizable + Available Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Whether you can cover short-term debts using cash and near-cash assets, without needing to sell inventory to do it.
Why it matters: it's a stricter, faster test than working capital alone, useful for catching a cash crunch before it becomes a crisis rather than after.
Healthy range: generally, at or above 1.0 is considered safe, meaning you could cover current liabilities without relying on inventory sales. Software and service businesses, which hold little inventory, often run comfortably above this.

Cash generation: is the business actually making money, or just recording it

2. Cash Flow
How am I doing on cash generation?
Cash Flow = Net Profit + Depreciation & Amortization
The real cash a business generates, adding back non-cash charges that reduce accounting profit but never leave the bank account.
Why it matters: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. This shows whether the business is actually generating money to reinvest or pay down debt, not just recording a profit on paper.
Healthy direction: positive, and ideally growing faster than your burn rate. A pre-profitability startup will often show negative cash flow; the number to watch is whether the gap is narrowing on a predictable trajectory.

Profitability: are your investments and your capital paying off

3. Return on Assets (ROA)
Are my investments profitable?
ROA = EBIT ÷ Total Assets
How much operating profit the company generates for every euro invested in assets, before financing costs and taxes.
Why it matters: it isolates operating performance from how the business is financed, so you can judge whether the core activity itself is a good investment, independent of your capital structure.
Healthy direction: positive and trending up. Absolute "good" values vary enormously by industry and by capital-intensity, so track your own trend quarter over quarter rather than comparing directly against unrelated businesses.
4. Return on Equity (ROE)
Am I giving my partners enough return?
ROE = Net Profit ÷ Equity
The return generated on the capital that owners and shareholders have actually put into the business.
Why it matters: investors compare this against what they could earn elsewhere. If ROE consistently trails a safer alternative, it becomes harder to justify keeping, or adding, capital in your company.
Healthy direction: most early-stage startups run a negative ROE by design, since they're spending investor capital to build before earning it back. What matters is a credible, evidenced path toward a positive figure that beats what capital could earn elsewhere.

Collections and payments: how well you manage the timing of cash

5. DSO (Days Sales Outstanding)
Am I managing collections well?
DSO = Average Accounts Receivable ÷ Operating Revenue × 365
The average number of days it takes to collect payment from customers after a sale.
Why it matters: the longer this is, the more of your own cash is tied up financing your customers instead of your own operations. Shortening it frees up working capital without needing new financing.
Healthy range: varies by business model, but for most B2B SaaS and service businesses, under 45-60 days is a reasonable target. A rising DSO over consecutive quarters is usually a more useful signal than the absolute number itself.
6. DPO (Days Payable Outstanding)
Am I managing payments well?
DPO = Average Accounts Payable ÷ Net Purchases × 365
The average number of days your company takes to pay its own suppliers.
Why it matters: a healthy DPO lets you use supplier credit as a source of free financing. Push it too far, though, and you risk damaging supplier relationships and losing early-payment terms.
Healthy direction: roughly matched to, or modestly higher than, your DSO. If you're paying suppliers faster than you're collecting from customers, you're financing that gap out of your own cash reserves.

Financial structure: how much of the business is borrowed

8. Debt Ratio (Leverage)
Is my financial structure correct?
Debt Ratio = Liabilities ÷ (Liabilities + Equity)
The proportion of the company financed by debt versus by owners' capital.
Why it matters: too much debt increases financial risk and interest burden; too little can mean you're not using leverage that could otherwise help you grow. Investors and lenders both use this to gauge how risky your balance sheet is.
Healthy range: a debt ratio below roughly 0.5-0.6 is generally viewed as conservative for an early-stage company. Most venture-backed startups run low on this ratio simply because equity, not debt, funds the early years.
9. Debt Repayment Capacity
Can I negotiate with the banks?
Debt Repayment Capacity = (Profit + Depreciation & Amortization) ÷ Liabilities
How much of your total debt the business could repay using the cash it generates in a single year.
Why it matters: banks use this to decide how much more they're willing to lend you, and on what terms. A strong ratio gives you real negotiating leverage the next time you need financing.
Healthy direction: higher is better; a ratio that suggests you could repay your debt load within a small number of years signals real capacity, not just current solvency.

Efficiency: how hard your assets are working for you

10. Total Asset Turnover
Are my investments efficient?
Asset Turnover = Sales ÷ Total Assets
How much revenue the company generates for every euro tied up in assets.
Why it matters: it shows how efficiently the business uses what it owns to generate sales. Low turnover can point to idle or underused assets sitting on the balance sheet.
Healthy direction: higher is generally better, but the "right" number depends heavily on how asset-heavy your business model is. A services or software business with few physical assets should show a much higher turnover than a hardware or inventory-heavy one.
How to use these on a financials slide, not just in a spreadsheet
You don't need to present all ten. Pick the two or three most relevant to your business model, for most SaaS startups that's usually cash flow, DSO, and debt ratio, and show them as a short trend line over the last few quarters. A ratio moving in the right direction over time is more convincing than a single snapshot, good or bad.
A note on "healthy" ranges

Every benchmark above is a general reference point, not a rule. Healthy ranges shift by industry, by geography, and by business model, a hardware company and a SaaS company will never look alike on asset turnover, and that's expected. What investors are actually evaluating is whether you understand what's driving each ratio and have a plan for the ones that aren't yet where you'd like them to be.

Key takeaways
  • These ten ratios cover four areas: liquidity, cash generation, profitability, and efficiency. Together they answer whether the business is healthy, not just growing.
  • You don't need every ratio to be strong today. You need to know where you stand and have a plan for the ones that aren't.
  • Working capital and the quick ratio test whether you can meet short-term obligations; both should stay positive.
  • DSO and DPO measure the timing of cash in and out; a rising DSO trend is usually more telling than the number itself.
  • Pick the two or three ratios most relevant to your business model for your slide, and show the trend, not just a single snapshot.
