How to read the Seed Business model checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Seed, the Business model checklist stops accepting estimates and starts demanding history. You've had customers for a while now, so the numbers this checklist wants should already exist somewhere in your billing system.

The Seed Business model checklist has four lines. Each one is testing whether your unit economics and revenue logic are built from actual customer behavior over time, not rough guesses extended forward. This guide walks through all four: what each line is really verifying, the common way founders half-satisfy it, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Real CAC, LTV, and payback period data included

This line is checking for actuals, not projections dressed up as actuals. Real CAC means fully loaded spend divided by customers actually acquired in a defined period, real LTV means it's built from observed retention and expansion, not an assumed churn rate, and payback period needs to be stated in months with the math shown.

Common miss:
Quoting a single blended CAC number without segmenting by channel, so the number hides that one channel is highly profitable and another is burning cash underneath the average.
What "done" looks like:
"Blended CAC is $410, but outbound sales CAC is $290 with 11-month payback, while paid social CAC is $890 with 26-month payback, which is why we're reallocating budget toward outbound."
2
Revenue model stated clearly (subscription, transactional, etc.)

This line is checking that your revenue model is named precisely, including any blend of models, and that a reviewer can predict how revenue moves as usage changes. "We charge for the product" is not a revenue model, subscription, usage-based, transactional take rate, and hybrid structures each behave completely differently as customers scale.

Common miss:
Describing pricing tiers ("Starter, Growth, Enterprise") without stating the underlying mechanic, so a reviewer can't tell if revenue is flat monthly, usage-linked, or transaction-based.
What "done" looks like:
"Hybrid model: a $199/month base subscription plus a 1.5% transaction fee on payment volume processed, which is why revenue per account grew 40% without a price increase."
3
Path to profitability or positive unit economics shown

This line is checking whether you can show the specific levers, and roughly when each one kicks in, that move you from current burn to breakeven or to durably positive unit economics. It's not asking for company-wide profitability yet, but it is asking for a named mechanism, not a hopeful trend line.

Common miss:
A hockey-stick revenue chart that crosses a flat cost line at some future date, with no named lever (pricing change, CAC reduction, gross margin improvement) explaining why costs stay flat while revenue grows.
What "done" looks like:
"Gross margin per account improves from 61% to 74% as support costs amortize past month 6; combined with our current 118% NRR, we reach contribution-margin breakeven by Q3 2027 without new hires."
4
Pricing strategy explained, not just current pricing

This line is checking for a strategy, meaning the logic behind how and when price changes as the company grows, not a static price sheet. Reviewers want to know what triggers a price increase, a new tier, or a packaging change, and what evidence supports that plan.

Common miss:
Listing current tiers and prices with no explanation of what happens next, leaving the impression that today's price sheet is permanent and untested for headroom.
What "done" looks like:
"We've raised list price twice in 14 months (from $149 to $199) with under 2% logo churn each time, and we're testing a usage-based add-on tier next based on the 30% of accounts already exceeding base plan limits."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • Single blended CAC number, no channel breakdown, no visible payback math
  • Pricing tiers listed with no stated mechanic (subscription vs. usage vs. transactional)
  • Hockey-stick chart crossing a flat cost line, no named lever explaining the gap
  • Current price sheet shown with no explanation of what triggers a future change
Actually done
  • "$290 outbound CAC / 11mo payback vs. $890 paid social / 26mo payback" (segmented)
  • "$199/mo base + 1.5% transaction fee" (mechanic named, tied to actual growth)
  • "Margin 61% to 74% by month 6, breakeven Q3 2027 without new hires" (levers named)
  • "Raised list price twice, under 2% churn each time, testing usage add-on next" (strategy, evidenced)

The pattern across all four: at Seed, the Business model checklist rewards founders who can segment their own numbers rather than blend them, because the blend is usually where the weak channel hides.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: if I split this number by channel, cohort, or account segment, would the story still hold, or would it fall apart into one good number and one bad one hiding behind an average? Real unit economics survive being split apart. Estimates usually don't.

For the mechanics of building real CAC, LTV, and a path to profitability, see How to build real CAC, LTV and a path to profitability, or work through the Unit Economics & Path to Profitability Worksheet directly.

Key takeaways
  • CAC, LTV, and payback period need to be real and segmented by channel, not a single blended estimate.
  • Name the exact revenue mechanic (subscription, usage, transactional, or a stated hybrid), not just tier names.
  • A path to profitability needs a named lever and rough timing, not a hockey-stick chart with an implied gap.
  • Pricing strategy is about what triggers future changes, not just what today's price sheet says.
  • Numbers that survive being split by channel or cohort are the ones that actually hold up under review.
