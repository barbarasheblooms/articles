How to read the Series A Solution checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Series A, the solution slide stops being about whether the product works and starts being about whether it keeps working as the company gets much bigger. That shift changes what every line on this checklist is actually testing.

The Series A solution checklist has four lines. Each one asks the founder to project the current product forward: does the fit hold at scale, does the moat hold at scale, does the roadmap protect the moat, does the experience hold under real load. Here's what each line is really checking.

1
Product-market fit demonstrated with evidence, not asserted

This line checks for behavioral proof, not sentiment. Customer quotes and NPS scores measure how people feel about the product; retention, expansion, and organic pull measure what people actually do with it, and only the second kind of evidence tells an investor whether fit is real.

Common miss:
Citing a strong NPS score or a handful of glowing customer quotes as proof of fit, without showing whether usage is actually growing, expanding, or sticking over time.
What "done" looks like:
"92% net dollar retention across 140 port customers over the last 12 months, plus 30% of new customers arrived through inbound referral with no sales outreach."
2
Defensibility at scale explained: what stays hard to copy as you grow

This line checks whether the moat is durable, not just present today. Something that's defensible at ten customers can evaporate at two hundred once a well-funded competitor shows up, so the founder needs to explain the specific mechanism that gets stronger, not weaker, as the company scales.

Common miss:
Pointing to "first mover advantage" or "strong customer relationships" as the moat, neither of which actually becomes harder to replicate as competitors enter with more capital and time.
What "done" looks like:
"Every port on Harborline contributes berth-scheduling data that trains our congestion-prediction model. At 140 ports, our forecast accuracy is 34% better than a new entrant's would be on day one, and that gap widens with each port added."
3
Roadmap shown for sustaining the advantage, not just adding features

This line checks whether the roadmap actively widens the moat described in the line above, rather than just growing the surface area of the product. A roadmap full of customer-requested features can be a healthy sign of listening to users, but it doesn't, by itself, defend the advantage that got the company here.

Common miss:
Presenting a roadmap made up entirely of customer-requested features (custom reports, more integrations) with nothing tied to strengthening the actual defensibility mechanism.
What "done" looks like:
"Next 12 months: expand data partnerships with three regional customs agencies to enrich the prediction model, which compounds the data moat, alongside standard feature requests like custom reporting."
4
User experience proven to hold up under scale

This line checks whether the product experience that worked for early adopters, who tend to tolerate rough edges, still works when usage volume, account complexity, or user count grows by ten times. Scale often breaks things that felt perfectly fine at small numbers, and investors want to see that tested, not assumed.

Common miss:
Demoing the product using a single small customer's data, which loads fast and looks clean, without showing performance at the size of the company's largest current account.
What "done" looks like:
"Load-tested the scheduling dashboard with our largest port's actual volume, 2,200 vessel calls a month. Page load stayed under 1.2 seconds, and support tickets per active user actually dropped 18% quarter over quarter as we scaled from 40 to 140 ports."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Our NPS score is 62." (sentiment, not behavior; doesn't show growth or stickiness)
  • "We have first mover advantage." (doesn't get harder to copy as competitors enter with capital)
  • Roadmap made entirely of customer-requested features with no link to the moat.
  • Product demoed only with one small, clean customer account.
Actually done
  • "92% net dollar retention across 140 customers; 30% of new customers arrive via inbound referral."
  • "Every port contributes data that improves our prediction model; the gap widens with scale."
  • "Data partnerships that compound the moat, alongside standard feature requests."
  • "Load-tested at our largest customer's real volume; load times and support load both held."

The pattern across all four: every claim needs to answer "and does that still hold at 10x the size?" Fit, defensibility, roadmap, and UX all get re-tested at Series A against a much larger hypothetical company than the one that exists today.

A useful test

For each claim on your solution slide, ask: is this true because of something structural that grows with scale, or is it true today because the company is still small enough that problems haven't shown up yet? If it's the second, say so and show the plan to fix it before an investor finds the gap first.

Key takeaways
  • Prove product-market fit with behavioral evidence (retention, expansion, organic pull), not sentiment scores.
  • Explain the specific mechanism that makes your moat harder to copy as you grow, not just today.
  • Your roadmap should visibly strengthen that moat, not just add features customers happen to ask for.
  • Test and show that the user experience holds up at your largest customer's actual volume, not a clean demo account.
  • Every Series A solution claim should hold up when scaled 10x, not just at current size.
