How to read the Seed Team checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Seed, the team checklist stops asking who you are and starts asking who you're becoming. It wants proof the team is already growing into the company you're raising to build, not a promise that it will.

The Seed team checklist has four lines. Together they test whether hiring, technical capacity, and advisory support are keeping pace with the company's ambitions, or trailing behind them. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
How the team has grown since launch explained

This line is checking whether hiring decisions have followed a clear logic tied to what the business actually needed at each stage, not just headcount added because the company could afford it. A real answer traces specific hires to specific problems they were brought in to solve.

Common miss:
"We've grown from 3 to 12 people," a headcount number with no explanation of which roles were added, in what order, or why.
What "done" looks like:
Nimbus Freight (a logistics matching platform) grew from 2 founders to 9 people in 14 months: first two backend engineers to handle carrier-side matching load, then an ops lead once daily shipments passed 300, then two account managers once carrier churn became the top complaint in exit surveys.
2
Technical capacity to scale the product addressed

This is checking whether the engineering team has explicitly reasoned about what breaks at 10x current volume, and whether they're already working on it, rather than assuming the current architecture will simply keep up. It wants a specific bottleneck named, not a general assurance that the team is "strong."

Common miss:
"Our engineering team is highly capable and has built scalable systems before," a claim about the team's pedigree with no reference to this product's actual scaling constraints.
What "done" looks like:
Nimbus Freight's matching algorithm currently re-computes carrier assignments in batches every 90 seconds, which held fine through 300 daily shipments but showed queuing delays past 800; the team has a rewrite to event-driven matching scoped and half-built, targeted to ship before shipment volume crosses 1,000/day.
3
2-3 critical hires planned post-raise, with timelines

This line is checking for focus: a short, specific list of the roles that will most directly unblock growth, each with a timeline, rather than a long wish list of every role that would be nice to have. It signals the founders know exactly what's constraining them right now.

Common miss:
A hiring plan listing 8 to 10 roles across every department with no prioritization or sequencing, making it unclear which hires actually unblock growth.
What "done" looks like:
Nimbus Freight's plan: VP Engineering within 90 days of close to own the matching rewrite and free the CTO for architecture, a Head of Carrier Ops within 60 days to reduce the 11% monthly carrier churn, and a senior data scientist within 120 days to build predictive ETAs, the top requested feature from enterprise shipper prospects.
4
Advisors with relevant domain expertise listed

This is checking whether the advisory bench has shifted from general startup mentors toward people with specific expertise in the operational problems the company is now facing, since the kind of help a company needs changes between Pre-Seed and Seed. Domain-specific counsel on a hard problem is worth more here than broad encouragement.

Common miss:
Keeping the same general startup-mentor advisors from Pre-Seed without adding anyone who has specific experience in the operational problems now emerging, like carrier logistics or freight marketplace dynamics.
What "done" looks like:
Nimbus Freight added Tomas Bergman, a former VP of Network Operations at a national freight brokerage, who reviews carrier churn data monthly and has directly introduced the team to two enterprise shipper prospects now in active pilots.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "We've grown from 3 to 12 people" (headcount with no explanation of which roles or why)
  • "Our engineering team is highly capable and has built scalable systems before" (pedigree, not this product's actual bottleneck)
  • "We plan to hire across engineering, sales, ops, and marketing" (unprioritized list, unclear what unblocks growth)
  • Same general startup-mentor advisors carried over from Pre-Seed (no new domain-specific expertise added)
Actually done
  • "2 backend engineers for matching load, then an ops lead at 300 shipments/day, then 2 AMs once churn spiked" (hires tied to specific triggers)
  • "Matching queues at 800+ shipments/day; event-driven rewrite half-built, shipping before 1,000/day" (specific bottleneck, work in progress)
  • "VP Engineering (90 days), Head of Carrier Ops (60 days), Senior Data Scientist (120 days)" (3 roles, sequenced, timed)
  • "Former VP Network Ops at a national freight brokerage reviews churn monthly, introduced 2 enterprise pilots" (specific expertise, measurable impact)

The pattern across all four: at Seed, investors want to see that hiring, engineering, and advisory decisions are being driven by specific, named constraints in the business, not by a general sense that the company should be getting bigger.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: can I name the specific trigger, bottleneck, or gap this decision responds to, and the number behind it? If the answer is a headcount total, a general claim of capability, an unordered wish list, or a recycled advisor name, the line isn't done yet.

Key takeaways
  • Team growth should be explained as hires tied to specific triggers, not a headcount number.
  • Technical scaling claims need a named bottleneck and evidence of work already underway, not a pedigree claim.
  • Post-raise hiring plans should be 2-3 roles, sequenced and timed, not a long unprioritized wish list.
  • Advisors should carry domain expertise matched to the company's current operational problems.
  • Investors are checking whether growth decisions follow specific evidence, not general ambition.
