Business Model

What makes a business model good, and why investors check it first

A business model is not your product. It is the explanation of how your company turns activity into money, and it is usually the first thing an experienced investor tests for holes.

Founders often use "business model" and "product" as if they were the same thing. They are not. Your product is what customers use. Your business model is the answer to a much narrower question: who pays, how much, how often, and why that arrangement gets more profitable as you grow. Investors read the rest of your pitch through this lens, because a great product with a weak business model is a feature, and a mediocre product with a strong business model is a company.

01The four questions a business model has to answer

Strip away the frameworks and a business model is really just four questions, answered in order.

The core questions

  • Who pays? Not "who uses it," who pays. In many venture businesses these are different people, and conflating them is one of the most common early mistakes.
  • What are they paying for? The specific value being exchanged for money, stated in terms the payer would use, not in terms of your feature list.
  • How much, and how often? A price and a frequency. This is what turns a value proposition into a revenue line.
  • Why does this get more efficient with scale? The cost of serving customer 1,000 should be lower, proportionally, than the cost of serving customer 1. If it is not, you have a services business, not a scalable one, and that changes what kind of capital and growth plan makes sense.

02Why investors look here first

Investors are not evaluating whether your idea is good. They are evaluating whether the economics of the idea can support a venture-scale outcome. A business model tells them three things at a glance: how predictable your revenue will be, how much it costs to acquire and keep a customer relative to what that customer is worth, and how much capital you will need before the model works on its own.

A pitch can have a compelling problem, a well-designed product, and a large market, and still get a pass if the business model implies the company will always be spending more to get a customer than that customer is worth. Market size describes the ceiling. Business model describes whether you can profitably climb toward it.

03The Business Model Canvas, briefly

The most widely used tool for mapping a business model is the Business Model Canvas, developed by Alexander Osterwalder. It breaks a company into nine building blocks: who you serve, what you offer them, how you reach and keep them, how you make money, and what it costs and requires to run the whole thing. The right side of the canvas is about creating and delivering value; the left side is about the resources, work, and partners required to make that happen; revenue and cost sit at the bottom as the financial outcome of everything above.

SheBlooms has a fillable version of the canvas in the Business Model resources tab, matched to this exact framework, with a completeness check built in. It is the fastest way to see your whole model on one page and spot the block that is thinnest.

04Signs your business model is not yet real

Common gaps

  • The pricing is theoretical. No one outside your team and friends has actually paid the number you are using in your model.
  • The unit economics are aspirational. Your CAC and LTV are estimates of what they will be "at scale," not what they are today, with no bridge explaining how you get from here to there.
  • The revenue model has not been chosen. You describe the product clearly but cannot say in one sentence whether you charge a subscription, a transaction fee, a license, or something else.
  • Margins are undefined. You know your price but not your cost to deliver, so you cannot say whether growth improves or worsens your cash position.

None of these are disqualifying at Pre-Seed. They are disqualifying if you present them as settled when they are still assumptions. The fix is not more polish, it is more honesty about what is proven versus estimated, paired with a plan to close the gap.

05What "good" looks like at each stage

StageWhat investors expect to see
Pre-SeedPricing tested with real people, even a handful, and a rough, honest CAC/LTV estimate with the assumptions shown.
SeedA clearly named revenue model, real CAC and LTV from actual data, and a credible, if early, path to profitability.
Series AGross margin benchmarked against your category, net revenue retention, and proof the unit economics hold as you scale, not just today.

A business model is a living document, not a slide you write once. The founders who handle this well treat their canvas the way they treat their financial model: something to revisit every time a new cohort of data comes in, not something to defend unchanged from the first draft.
