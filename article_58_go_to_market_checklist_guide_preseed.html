How to read the Pre-Seed Go-to-market checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Pre-Seed, the go-to-market checklist looks like it's asking for a growth strategy. It's actually asking for proof that you spent real money or real hours on one channel and know exactly what happened, not a slide of channels you're excited to try.

The Pre-Seed go-to-market checklist has four lines. Each one is easy to fill with intentions and hard to fill with evidence, which is exactly the gap investors are checking for. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Primary acquisition channel identified and tested for real

This line isn't checking whether you can name a channel, almost every founder can. It's checking whether you've actually run it: real spend, real outreach, or real hours, with a result you can point to. "Identified" is the easy half. "Tested for real" is the part that gets skipped, because a plan to try something after the raise feels like progress but produces zero evidence.

Common miss:
Naming three or four channels that all sound plausible (SEO, referrals, paid social, partnerships) without having spent a dollar or an hour on any single one of them yet.
What "done" looks like:
Renee Ibarra, founder of Fixify (on-demand appliance repair booking for renters), ran $1,800 in Instagram DM ads across two Chicago zip codes over 5 weeks: 340 clicks, 41 completed bookings, tracked through her scheduling software rather than ad-platform "conversions."
2
CAC calculated for that channel

This is checking whether your cost-per-customer number is fully loaded, meaning it includes tools and labor, and whether it's measured against paying customers rather than leads or signups. A CAC that only counts ad spend divided by clicks flatters every channel, which is exactly why it doesn't survive diligence.

Common miss:
Reporting ad spend divided by signups or trial starts, not paying customers, and leaving out the time a founder or contractor spent managing the channel.
What "done" looks like:
Renee's CAC: $1,800 ad spend plus $220 paid to a part-time assistant managing DM replies, divided by 41 completed (paid) bookings, equals $49.27 per customer, against an average order value of $95.
3
Plan to scale that channel stated

This line is checking whether you've thought about what happens when you spend more, not just whether you intend to. Does CAC hold, rise, or does the channel have a ceiling because the audience is a fixed size? A credible plan says something specific about that drift and what it requires operationally.

Common miss:
"We'll just put more budget behind it once we raise," with no view on whether CAC holds, how big the addressable audience is, or what breaks operationally at higher volume.
What "done" looks like:
Renee's plan: expand from $1,800 to $5,000 a month across three additional Chicago neighborhoods, budgeting for CAC to drift from $49 to roughly $65 as she moves past her warmest audience segment, and hiring a part-time community manager once monthly bookings pass 60.
4
Learnings from channels that did not work included

This is checking for evidence of real experimentation, not a lucky guess dressed up as strategy. A founder who can explain precisely why two other channels failed has clearly run a process. A founder who only shows the one channel that worked may just be describing the one thing they happened to try.

Common miss:
Leaving failed channels out entirely, so the winning channel reads as obvious or inevitable in hindsight rather than the result of testing several options.
What "done" looks like:
Renee also tested door-hanger flyers in two apartment buildings (200 flyers, 3 bookings, CAC $310) and cold calls to property managers (12 calls, 0 conversions in 3 weeks), and can explain why: flyers had no urgency trigger, and property manager sales cycles ran longer than her runway allowed.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "We're planning to test SEO, referrals, and paid social" (a list of intentions, not a tested channel)
  • "CAC is about $15 per signup" (counts signups, not paying customers, and ignores labor cost)
  • "We'll just put more budget behind it once we raise" (no view on whether CAC holds as spend rises)
  • "Paid ads didn't really work for us" (no data, no reason, no proof another channel was actually tried)
Actually done
  • "$1,800 in Instagram DM ads over 5 weeks: 340 clicks, 41 completed bookings" (real spend, tracked outcome)
  • "$1,800 spend + $220 assistant time / 41 bookings = $49.27 fully loaded CAC" (labor included, measured against paying customers)
  • "Scaling to $5,000/month, budgeting CAC to rise to ~$65 past our warmest segment" (addresses saturation directly)
  • "Flyers returned 3 bookings from 200 ($310 CAC); property manager calls converted 0 of 12" (specific, falsifiable, with a stated reason)

The pattern across all four: one channel with real numbers outranks four channels with none. Investors reading this line aren't grading ambition, they're checking whether a founder has already run a small, honest experiment and can describe it in enough detail that it could be replicated.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: is there a number attached that came from an actual campaign, list, or call log, not a guess? Could you show the spreadsheet, the ad account, or the outreach tracker behind it? If the answer describes a plan rather than a result, the line isn't done yet, no matter how confident it sounds.

Key takeaways
  • A real test beats a plausible list: one channel with data outranks four channels with none.
  • CAC must be fully loaded (spend plus tools plus time) and measured against paying customers, not leads.
  • A scaling plan should state what happens to CAC as spend increases, not just "we'll spend more."
  • Failed channels are evidence of real experimentation; list them with numbers and reasons, not silence.
  • Investors are looking for one proven, specific loop, not breadth of untested options.
