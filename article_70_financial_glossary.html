The founder's financial glossary: cash flow, runway, burn rate and the other terms you'll be tested on

By SheBlooms 10 min read

Investors decide how seriously to take your financials in the first thirty seconds, often based on nothing more than whether you use the right word for the right number. This is the vocabulary that makes that first thirty seconds work in your favor.

Most founders learn financial vocabulary backwards: a term shows up in a term sheet, an investor update, or a due diligence request, and only then do you go look it up. That works, eventually, but it means you're always one step behind in the room, translating on the fly while the conversation moves on without you.

This glossary exists so you don't have to do that. It's organized the way these terms actually show up for a founder, not alphabetically: first the numbers that decide whether you survive, then the ones that show whether you're growing, then the ones that prove the business actually works, and finally the balance-sheet vocabulary that shows up once, usually in diligence, and that almost nobody explains clearly the first time.

You don't need to memorize this. Bookmark it, and come back whenever a term shows up in an investor update, a board deck, or a question you didn't fully understand in the moment.

Cash: the language that decides whether you survive

Every other number in this glossary is secondary to these three. A startup can be unprofitable, pre-revenue, and early, and still be perfectly investable. A startup that has run out of cash is not a startup anymore.

Burn Rate
The amount of money your startup spends each month, net of whatever revenue comes in, before you reach profitability.
Why it matters: burn rate is the number your runway is built on. Investors will ask for it directly, and they will sanity-check it against your bank balance and your monthly expenses, so it needs to be a number you can defend on the spot, not a rough guess.
Runway
The amount of time your startup can keep operating before running out of cash, based on your current burn rate.
Why it matters: runway is the clock that determines when you need to close your next round, and it directly shapes how much leverage you have in that negotiation. Founders with six months of runway negotiate very differently than founders with eighteen.
Cash Flow
The actual movement of cash into and out of your business over a given period, adding back non-cash charges like depreciation that reduce accounting profit but never leave your bank account.
Why it matters: profit is an accounting opinion; cash is a fact. A company can show a profit on its income statement and still be dangerously low on cash, or the reverse. Cash flow is the number that tells you which one is actually happening.
Working Capital
The difference between your current assets (cash, inventory, receivables) and your current liabilities (what you owe within the next year).
Why it matters: a positive number means you can comfortably meet what you owe over the next twelve months. A negative one is an early warning sign of cash strain, sometimes even while the company looks profitable on paper.

Growth: the numbers investors listen for first

These are the metrics that show up in the first slide of almost every pitch deck, and the ones investors will use to benchmark you against every other company they've seen this quarter.

MRR Monthly Recurring Revenue
The predictable revenue your business collects every month from recurring subscriptions or contracts.
Why it matters: for subscription and SaaS businesses, MRR is the single most-watched number, because it isolates the predictable, repeatable part of your revenue from one-off deals that won't recur next month.
Gross Margin
The percentage of revenue left over after subtracting the direct cost of delivering your product or service (hosting, payment processing, cost of goods, direct labor).
Why it matters: gross margin shows how much of every euro of revenue is actually available to cover the rest of the business, salaries, marketing, product. A growth number without a gross margin next to it tells investors nothing about whether that growth is worth having.
LTV Customer Lifetime Value
The total revenue a typical customer is expected to generate over the full length of their relationship with your business.
Why it matters: LTV is what tells you, and your investors, how much you can afford to spend acquiring a new customer. Growth that costs more to acquire than it's worth over time isn't growth, it's a leak.

Profitability: proving the business actually works

Growth gets you attention. These are the numbers that convince an investor the business underneath the growth is sound.

Break-even Point
The point at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business is neither making a profit nor losing money.
Why it matters: knowing your break-even point, and being able to show a credible path to it, tells investors you understand exactly what has to happen for the business to sustain itself without continued external funding.
EBITDA Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation & Amortization
A measure of operating profitability that strips out the effects of financing decisions, tax structure, and non-cash accounting charges.
Why it matters: investors use EBITDA to judge how the core business performs on its own merits, independent of how it's financed or which country's tax code it operates under, which makes it easier to compare against other companies.
Profitability
Your company's ability to generate profit after covering all of its costs and expenses.
Why it matters: sustainable profitability, even if it's years away, is what ultimately justifies the valuation investors are paying today. It's the destination every projection is implicitly arguing you can reach.

Returns: how investors judge whether your capital is working

These three ratios sound similar and get confused constantly. Each one answers a different question, and mixing them up in front of an investor is one of the fastest ways to lose credibility on a financials slide.

ROI Return on Investment
The profitability of a specific investment or spend, measured against what it cost.
Why it matters: ROI is the most general of the three, and the most commonly used to justify a specific decision, a marketing channel, a hire, a piece of equipment, rather than the business as a whole.
ROA Return on Assets
How much operating profit the company generates for every euro invested in assets, before financing costs and taxes.
Why it matters: ROA isolates how well the core operation performs, independent of how it's financed, so it answers "is this a good business" rather than "is this well-financed."
ROE Return on Equity
The return generated on the capital that owners and shareholders have actually put into the business.
Why it matters: investors compare your ROE against what they could earn putting that same money somewhere safer. If ROE consistently trails a safer alternative, it gets harder to justify keeping capital in your company.
Telling these three apart, in one sentence each
ROI asks "was this specific spend worth it?" ROA asks "is this business good at turning what it owns into profit?" ROE asks "is this a good use of the owners' money, compared to their next best option?"

The balance sheet: what you own and what you owe

This vocabulary rarely comes up in a pitch, and shows up constantly in due diligence. Knowing it before you're asked is the difference between sounding prepared and sounding like you're learning it live.

Assets
Everything your business owns that has financial value: cash, inventory, equipment, and intellectual property among them.
Why it matters: assets are the resources your startup actually has to operate and grow with, as distinct from revenue, which is what you're generating from using them.
Liabilities
The financial obligations your company owes to others: loans, supplier invoices, and taxes among them.
Why it matters: liabilities represent future cash outflows you need to plan for. Every ratio dealing with financial risk is, in some form, comparing your liabilities to what you have available to cover them.
Equity
The value that belongs to the owners or shareholders once liabilities are subtracted from total assets.
Why it matters: equity is the owners' actual stake in the business, and it's the pool that every new investor's check adds to, and every round of dilution divides differently.
Current Assets & Current Liabilities
Assets expected to convert to cash within a year (cash, inventory, receivables), and obligations due within that same year (supplier payments, taxes, short-term loans).
Why it matters: the "current" distinction is what separates your near-term financial picture from your long-term one, and it's the basis for working capital and every liquidity ratio.
Liquidity
Your business's ability to pay its short-term financial obligations as they come due.
Why it matters: a business can be profitable on paper and still fail because it can't convert what it owns into cash fast enough to pay what it owes right now. Liquidity is what protects against that specific failure mode.
Solvency
Your business's ability to meet its long-term financial obligations, as distinct from the short-term focus of liquidity.
Why it matters: solvency is the longer horizon version of the same question liquidity asks: can this business meet what it owes, just measured over years instead of months.

Payables, receivables, and the mechanics of getting paid

Accounts Receivable
Money owed to your business by customers for products or services you've already delivered.
Why it matters: the longer this takes to collect, the more of your own cash is effectively financing your customers instead of your own operations.
Accounts Payable
Money your business owes to suppliers or creditors for goods and services you've already received.
Why it matters: managed well, accounts payable functions as a free, short-term source of financing. Managed poorly, it damages supplier relationships and the payment terms you're offered next time.
Depreciation
The gradual reduction in the recorded value of a physical asset over its useful life.
Why it matters: depreciation is a real cost of owning equipment, but it's non-cash, meaning it reduces your reported profit without actually taking money out of your bank account this month, which is why it gets added back when calculating cash flow.
Inventory Turnover
A measure of how frequently your inventory is sold and replaced over a given period.
Why it matters: for any startup that holds physical inventory, high turnover generally signals efficient inventory management and healthy demand; low turnover usually means cash sitting idle on a shelf.
Debt Ratio
The proportion of your company's assets that are financed through debt, rather than through owners' equity.
Why it matters: too much debt increases financial risk and interest burden; too little can mean you're not using leverage that could otherwise help you grow. Lenders and investors both use this to gauge how risky your balance sheet is.

The two statements you'll be asked for

Balance Sheet
A snapshot, at one specific point in time, of everything your company owns (assets), everything it owes (liabilities), and what's left for the owners (equity).
Why it matters: it's the fastest way for an investor to gauge your company's overall financial position and stability, independent of how any single month performed.
Income Statement Profit & Loss / P&L
A report summarizing revenue, expenses, and resulting profit or loss over a specific period, usually a month, quarter, or year.
Why it matters: where the balance sheet is a snapshot, the income statement is the story of a period, showing exactly where money was earned and where it went.
The one distinction worth memorizing above all others

Profit is an opinion, shaped by accounting choices like depreciation schedules and revenue recognition timing. Cash is a fact: it's either in the bank or it isn't. Whenever a financial term confuses you, ask which of the two it's actually measuring. It resolves more confusion than any other single question in this glossary.

Key takeaways
  • Burn rate, runway, and cash flow are the three numbers that decide whether you survive, and the ones you should be able to state without checking a spreadsheet.
  • MRR and gross margin together tell investors whether your growth is real and worth having, not just growth for its own sake.
  • ROI, ROA, and ROE sound alike but answer three different questions: was this spend worth it, is the business good, and is it a good use of the owners' money.
  • Balance sheet vocabulary rarely comes up in the pitch itself, but it comes up constantly in due diligence, so it's worth knowing before you're asked, not while you're being asked.
  • When in doubt about what a term is really measuring, ask whether it's about profit or about cash. Most confusion collapses once you know which one you're looking at.
