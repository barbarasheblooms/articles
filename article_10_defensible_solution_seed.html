Turning a shipped product into a defensible solution slide

By SheBlooms 8 min read

At Seed, "it's in development" stops being an acceptable answer. Investors expect a live product, a real roadmap, and a specific, evidenced reason it's hard to copy. Here's how to build that case.

The bar for the solution slide moves substantially between Pre-Seed and Seed. At Pre-Seed, a prototype and a credible roadmap were enough. At Seed, you have a shipped product with real usage, and investors expect the slide to reflect that: what's live, what it does today, and, critically, what stops a competitor from copying it once it's working.

Walk through what's actually shipped

The most common mistake at this stage is still leading with the roadmap instead of the product. If the product is live, show it live, a real walkthrough of the current version, not a curated highlight reel of planned features. Investors want to see the UX as customers experience it today, warts included.

Reads as Pre-Seed, not Seed
  • "Here's our vision for the platform" (mockups of unbuilt features)
  • Leading with the roadmap before showing what's live
  • No mention of what version is actually in production
Reads as Seed-ready
  • "This is v2.3, live since March, here's the core flow"
  • A specific list of shipped features, then the roadmap after
  • Honest notes on what's manual behind the scenes, if anything still is

Name your defensibility in one specific category

"Better UX" is not a moat, and investors at Seed have heard that line enough times to discount it automatically. A real defensibility argument names a specific mechanism and backs it with early evidence that it's actually forming.

1
Proprietary data
Data you accumulate from usage that makes your product better the longer customers use it, and that a new entrant can't replicate without the same usage history.
2
Network effects
The product gets more valuable to each user as more users join, whether that's a two-sided marketplace, shared content, or referral-driven growth.
3
Switching costs
The deeper a customer integrates your product into their workflow (data, configuration, team habits), the more costly it becomes for them to leave.
4
Technology or IP
A genuinely hard-to-replicate technical capability, patents, proprietary models, or infrastructure a competitor would need real time and capital to rebuild.

Pick the one (or two) that's actually true for your business, and show early evidence: retention curves that improve with tenure, a referral loop that's already generating signups, or integration depth data. A named mechanism with weak early evidence beats a vague claim with none.

Prove you're better than the workaround, with data

By Seed, "we're better than the old way of doing this" needs a comparison point that isn't just your own opinion. Usage data, time saved, retention versus the old tool, or direct customer statements when they explain why they switched, are the strongest forms of proof.

Weak evidence
"Customers love how much easier our product is to use."
Strong evidence
"71% of customers who previously used a spreadsheet for this task churned off it within their first month with us. Average setup time dropped from 3 days to 40 minutes."

The 12-month roadmap should read as a plan, not a wishlist

At Seed, the roadmap is no longer proving you can execute at all, it's proving you know which features deepen the moat rather than just adding surface area. Prioritise roadmap items that strengthen the defensibility mechanism you named, not just ones that sound impressive.

"Once we stopped saying 'better UX' and started saying 'our matching algorithm gets more accurate with every completed project, and we have three years of that data', the defensibility conversation changed completely."

SheBlooms founder
A structure that holds up at Seed

Live walkthrough of the shipped product. A short, specific feature and roadmap list for the next 12 months. One named defensibility mechanism with early evidence. One data point proving you're better than the workaround customers used before you.

Key takeaways
  • Lead with what's actually live and shipped, not with planned features.
  • Name one specific defensibility mechanism (data, network effects, switching costs, or technology) and back it with early evidence.
  • "Better UX" alone will not hold up as a moat at Seed.
  • Prove you're better than the prior workaround with usage data, not opinion.
  • The 12-month roadmap should deepen the moat, not just add features.
