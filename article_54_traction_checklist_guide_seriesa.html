How to read the Series A Traction checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Series A, the question is no longer whether you grew. It's whether that growth was a system that will keep working, or a lucky quarter that happened to land on your slide.

The Series A traction checklist has four lines, and each one is designed to rule out a lucky spike. A single great month proves almost nothing; three or four months of consistent, cohort-level evidence proves a business model is repeatable. This guide unpacks all four lines: what each is really verifying, the common way founders half-satisfy it, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
3+ months of consistent MoM growth shown (15-20%+ SaaS reference)

This line is checking for consistency, not a single strong data point. One 40% month can be a launch spike, a press hit, or a single large deal closing; three or four consecutive months near the reference range is what tells an investor the growth engine itself is working, not just one lever that got pulled once.

Common miss:
Leading with the single best month ("we grew 45% in March") and letting weaker surrounding months disappear from the slide.
What "done" looks like:
Northstar Ops, a workflow platform for regional trucking dispatchers, shows: "MRR grew 18%, 21%, 17%, and 19% across January through April," says founder Dana Whitfield, with every month plotted, not just the best one.
2
Net revenue retention stated (100%+ ideal)

This line is verifying whether your existing customer base is expanding or contracting in dollar terms, which is a stronger signal than logo retention alone. Net revenue retention above 100% means upsells and expansion are outrunning downgrades and churn, which tells an investor the product deepens in value over time rather than plateauing.

Common miss:
Quoting logo retention ("88% of customers stay") and calling it NRR, when the two numbers answer different questions and can move in opposite directions.
What "done" looks like:
"Northstar's net revenue retention is 112%: existing dispatch teams upgraded seat counts and added the routing module faster than we lost accounts," Dana reports, with the cohort math shown.
3
Monthly churn rate stated (below 5% SaaS reference)

This line is checking whether the growth number and the retention number are internally consistent, and whether churn is trending toward or away from the healthy reference range. Series A investors know that a business growing 20% a month with 12% churn is on a treadmill, not a rocket.

Common miss:
Reporting an annualized churn figure that sounds small ("we lose 9% of customers a year") without stating the monthly rate it implies, which can obscure a much rougher month-to-month picture.
What "done" looks like:
"Monthly logo churn is 3.4%, calculated across our 210 active accounts over the trailing 6 months," Dana states, plotted alongside the growth chart so the two numbers can be read together.
4
CAC, LTV and payback period backed by real, cohort-level data

This line is verifying that unit economics are built from actual cohorts, not a blended average that hides which channels or segments are actually profitable. Cohort-level data means grouping customers by acquisition month or channel and tracking their revenue and cost separately, so the numbers can be defended under questioning.

Common miss:
Presenting a single blended CAC and LTV across all channels and customer segments, which can mask a paid channel that's unprofitable and a referral channel that's carrying the average.
What "done" looks like:
"Our Q1 cohort: CAC of $1,850 for enterprise dispatch accounts, 14-month payback, projected LTV of $41,000 based on 34-month average lifetime," Dana's data room shows, broken out by acquisition channel and compared against three prior quarterly cohorts.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Grew 45% in March" (best month only, other months hidden)
  • "88% retention" labeled as net revenue retention
  • "9% annual churn" (obscures the monthly trend)
  • Single blended CAC/LTV across all channels and segments
Actually done
  • "18%, 21%, 17%, 19% MoM growth across four consecutive months"
  • "112% NRR, with upgrade and expansion math shown"
  • "3.4% monthly churn across 210 accounts, trailing 6 months"
  • "$1,850 CAC, 14-month payback, $41,000 LTV, by cohort and channel"

The pattern across all four: Series A diligence is designed to detect a lucky spike disguised as a system. Consistency across months, cohorts, and channels is what separates the two, and it's what every one of these four lines is quietly testing for.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: does this number hold up if I show three more months, or three more cohorts, next to it? If the answer is only sometimes, the metric is probably resting on a single good period rather than a repeatable system.

Key takeaways
  • Show every recent month's growth rate, not just the best one, to prove consistency.
  • Never substitute logo retention for net revenue retention; they answer different questions.
  • State monthly churn plainly, since annualized figures can hide a rougher trend.
  • Break CAC, LTV, and payback out by cohort and channel, not as one blended number.
  • Series A diligence is built to detect a lucky spike; consistency across periods is the antidote.
