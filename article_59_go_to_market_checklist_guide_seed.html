How to read the Seed Go-to-market checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Seed, the go-to-market checklist stops asking if you found a channel and starts asking if you found a business. It wants a small, repeatable engine with real numbers behind it, not a wider list of things that once worked once.

The Seed go-to-market checklist has four lines. Together they're checking whether growth is becoming a system rather than a series of one-off wins. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
2-3 core channels with real CAC and volume data

This line is checking for a portfolio, not a single lucky channel and not a scattershot list of everything you've tried. Two or three channels with real CAC and volume attached shows the growth isn't dependent on one platform's algorithm or one founder's personal network, and that you have enough data on each to trust the number.

Common miss:
Listing five or six channels that have each produced a handful of customers, none with enough volume to calculate a CAC anyone would trust.
What "done" looks like:
Lior Adamsky, founder of Ledgerly (bookkeeping software for freelancers), reports Google Search ads at $86 CAC on 210 customers over 4 months, an accounting-community affiliate program at $54 CAC on 140 customers, and an SEO blog at $31 CAC on 95 customers, each with enough volume to be a real average, not a fluke.
2
Channel mix and repeatability explained

This is checking whether you understand why each channel works and whether you can turn the crank again next month with predictable results, not just whether the channels happened to work last quarter. Repeatability means the mechanism is understood well enough to forecast, not just observed in hindsight.

Common miss:
Describing what happened last quarter without explaining the mechanism, so it's unclear whether next month's spend will produce a similar result or a different one.
What "done" looks like:
Lior can show that Search ads convert reliably because freelancers search "invoice software" with immediate intent, the affiliate program converts because partner communities trust the referring accountant, and both have held steady CAC within a 12% band for three consecutive months.
3
Payback period estimated per channel

This line is checking whether you know how long it takes to recover CAC in gross margin dollars, per channel, because a business can have great CAC and still run out of cash if payback stretches too long. It's a capital efficiency question, not a marketing metric.

Common miss:
Quoting a single blended payback period across all channels, which hides that one channel might pay back in 3 months and another in 14.
What "done" looks like:
Ledgerly's payback: Search ads pay back CAC in 4.1 months, the affiliate channel in 2.6 months, and the SEO blog in 5.8 months (slower because content and editing time is amortized into cost), all measured against a $19/month gross margin per customer.
4
Plan is to scale proven channels, not keep testing new ones

This is checking discipline: whether the raise is going toward doubling down on what already works, or toward more experimentation that pushes proof of a real business further out. At Seed, investors want to see conviction on the channels that are already producing, not a wider search.

Common miss:
Allocating a meaningful chunk of the new raise to "testing TikTok, podcasts, and a few new partnerships" instead of putting most of the budget behind the channels already proven.
What "done" looks like:
Lior's plan: 75% of the new marketing budget goes to scaling Search ads and the affiliate program from $9k to $30k a month combined, with a stated CAC ceiling of $115 before pausing further spend; the remaining 25% funds one new partnership channel with a hard 90-day test window.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "We're active on six channels" (too little volume per channel to trust any single CAC)
  • "These channels worked well last quarter" (no explanation of the mechanism behind the result)
  • "Payback is around 5 months on average" (a blended number that hides which channels are efficient)
  • "We'll keep testing new channels alongside our core ones" (dilutes focus away from what's already proven)
Actually done
  • "Search: $86 CAC/210 customers. Affiliate: $54 CAC/140. SEO: $31 CAC/95" (real volume behind each number)
  • "Search converts on intent, affiliate converts on partner trust; CAC held within 12% for 3 months" (mechanism plus stability)
  • "Search pays back in 4.1 months, affiliate in 2.6, SEO in 5.8" (per-channel, not blended)
  • "75% of new budget scales the two proven channels; 25% funds one new test with a 90-day window" (proven channels get priority)

The pattern across all four: at Seed, breadth of channels matters less than depth of understanding on the two or three that actually work. Investors reading this checklist are asking whether the next dollar of spend is going toward a known return or an open question.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: do I have enough volume on this channel to trust the average, and could I explain to a stranger why it works well enough that they'd believe it holds next quarter? If the answer relies on a single good month, the line isn't done yet.

Key takeaways
  • Two or three channels with real volume beat five or six with too little data to trust.
  • Repeatability means explaining the mechanism, not just reporting last quarter's result.
  • Payback should be calculated per channel, since a blended average hides which ones are actually efficient.
  • Most of the new raise should scale proven channels, not fund a wider search for new ones.
  • Investors are checking for depth of understanding on what works, not breadth of what's been tried.
