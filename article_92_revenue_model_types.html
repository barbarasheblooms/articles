Revenue model types: which one actually fits your business

The mechanism you choose for getting paid shapes your margins, your predictability, and how much capital you need. Here are the models founders use most, and how to tell which one fits.

"How do you make money" has a small number of real answers. Most companies use one primary mechanism, sometimes blended with a secondary one later. Picking the wrong one for your business is not fatal, but discovering it late is expensive, since it usually means rebuilding your pricing, your sales process, and your metrics at the same time.

01Subscription

Customers pay a recurring fee, usually monthly or annual, for ongoing access. This is the model investors default to liking, because it produces predictable, recurring revenue and, done well, very high gross margins.

Fits best when

  • The product delivers value continuously, not in a single moment.
  • Customers would notice and mind if access stopped.
  • You can keep the cost of serving each customer low as you add more of them.

Watch for: churn is the model's biggest risk. A subscription business with high monthly churn is not actually more predictable than a one-time sale business, it just delays when the volatility shows up.

02Transactional

You earn a fee each time a specific action happens: a purchase, a booking, a shipment. Revenue scales directly with usage rather than with a fixed subscriber base.

Fits best when usage is naturally variable and a flat subscription would either overcharge light users or undercharge heavy ones. The tradeoff is predictability: transactional revenue moves with customer behavior, seasonality, and market conditions in a way subscription revenue does not.

03Marketplace / take rate

You connect buyers and sellers and take a percentage of each transaction between them. This model does not require you to hold inventory or deliver the core service yourself, which is why it can scale quickly, but it depends entirely on having enough of both sides to make the marketplace worth using.

Reference pointsRide-hailing marketplaces commonly run a blended take rate in the 20 to 25 percent range; short-term rental marketplaces often run closer to 14 to 16 percent combined across both sides. Your own take rate should reflect how much value you add to the transaction, not just what the category average happens to be.

04Freemium

A free tier attracts users, and a paid tier monetizes the ones who need more. This model can produce fast top-of-funnel growth, but the entire model lives or dies on the free-to-paid conversion rate. A freemium business with a 1 percent conversion rate and a subscription business that only sells the paid tier can end up in a very similar place financially, except the freemium version carries the cost of serving everyone who never converts.

05Licensing

You charge for the right to use something you own: software, a patent, content, or a brand. Margins can be very high because the marginal cost of granting another license is close to zero, but growth is often gated by how many licensees you can find and negotiate with, which can make this model slower to scale than it looks on paper.

Many real companies blend models, for example a subscription core with transactional add-ons. That is fine, and often smart, as long as you can state which one is primary today and why the second one exists, rather than listing every mechanism you might someday use.

The right model is the one that matches how your customer already thinks about paying for value like yours, not the one that looks most impressive on a slide. A transactional model that mirrors how your customer already buys will out-convert a subscription model that asks them to change their habits, even if the subscription model has better margins on paper.
