How to run board meetings, size your option pool, and keep governance healthy as you grow

SheBloomsTeam & governance

Governance isn't the paperwork you deal with after the real work is done. Poorly run board meetings and an undersized option pool are two of the quietest ways a growing company loses time and talent.

Once you have a board and you're hiring beyond the founding team, two operational habits do more for the health of your company than almost anything else: running board meetings that actually produce decisions, and sizing your equity pool so you can make competitive offers without constantly renegotiating dilution.

Running a board meeting that's worth everyone's time

The most common failure mode is spending the entire meeting reciting numbers that were already in the pre-read. If your board is just watching you read your own deck out loud, you've wasted the one recurring block of time you have with the people whose input and approval you actually need.

A structure that works for most early-stage boards
  • Send materials 3-7 days ahead — enough time for directors to actually read them before the meeting, not skim them in the elevator.
  • Open with a short CEO snapshot (5 minutes) — what changed since last meeting, in plain language, before anyone dives into the deck.
  • Use a consent agenda for routine approvals — bundle option grants, minor policy updates, and other routine items into a single vote instead of walking through each one live. This alone can reclaim 15-20 minutes.
  • Spend the majority of the meeting on 2-3 real decisions — the things you actually need the board's judgment on, not updates they could have read.
  • Close with numbered action items and owners — and circulate minutes within a day or two while the discussion is still fresh.
SegmentTimeWhat happens
CEO snapshot5 minHeadline update, in your own words — not a re-read of the deck.
KPI & financial review15-20 minQuestions on the numbers already sent in advance, not first exposure to them.
Strategic discussion30-45 minThe 2-3 real decisions on the table — hiring, budget, a partnership, a pivot.
Closed session & action items10-15 minSensitive topics (comp, performance) and a final list of owners and deadlines.

Aim for 60-90 minutes total. A board meeting that regularly runs past two hours is usually a sign the pre-read wasn't doing its job.

Sizing your option pool

The option pool is the block of equity set aside — before it's granted to anyone — to make competitive offers to the people you'll hire next. Get it too small and you'll be back at the negotiating table asking your board (and diluting yourself again) every time you need to make a strong offer. Get it right once and you can hire confidently for 18-24 months without touching it.

StageTypical pool size (fully diluted)Why
Seed10-15%Covers early key hires — first engineers, first commercial hire — through to the next round.
Series A10-15%, often refreshed/expandedInvestors frequently require the pool be topped back up before the round closes, sized to your specific 18-24 month hiring plan rather than a flat default.
Who actually pays for the pool

Pool expansions required as a condition of a round are typically added before the new money comes in — which means the dilution is absorbed by existing shareholders (mostly the founders), not by the new investor. Model this before you agree to a pool size in a term sheet; a larger pool than you need is dilution you're taking on for equity that may sit unused.

Advisor equity, without giving away more than the relationship is worth

Advisors are not employees and shouldn't be priced like one. Typical ranges cluster by how active the relationship actually is:

  • Occasional/episodic advisors — roughly 0.25-1% equity, often vesting over 1-2 years.
  • Active, ongoing advisors (regular calls, real influence on decisions) — roughly 1-2%.
  • Whole advisory board combined — keep the total under about 1-2% of the cap table, not per person. An advisory board that adds up to 5-8% combined has usually been over-granted.

If an advisor asks for a board observer seat rather than equity alone, that's a reasonable, lower-stakes request than an actual board seat — just specify in writing that it's revocable and carries no vote. If an advisor asks for a formal board seat and equity above these ranges at an early stage, treat that as a negotiation, not a default.

This overview is educational and general in nature. Equity grants, vesting terms, and governance decisions should be reviewed with a startup lawyer and your existing investors before you finalize them.
