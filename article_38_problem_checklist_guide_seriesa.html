How to read the Series A problem checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 6 min read

At Series A, all four checklist lines are really one question asked four different ways: not "is the problem real," but "why does this window stay open long enough to justify this round." Here is what each line is actually checking for.

The Series A problem checklist looks short: four lines. But each one is a specific test of whether your timing argument is sound, and it's easy to tick a box with language that sounds right without the substance an investor is actually scoring behind it. This guide walks through all four lines individually.

1
Timing ("why now") argument stated, not the fundamentals restated

This line is checking that you've actually swapped arguments, not just softened the old one. "Why now" is a different claim than "why this problem." If your slide could have been shown at Seed with no changes, this line isn't done, no matter how well-written the problem description is.

Common miss:
Adding a sentence like "and this problem is more urgent than ever" to the end of an otherwise unchanged Seed-era problem slide, without a distinct timing claim behind it.
What "done" looks like:
A slide that leads with a dated trigger and a trend, not a restated pain point: "This window opened in [date] because [specific change], and it's still open because [specific trend]."
2
Structural shift identified: regulation, behavior change, or technology

This line is checking that your timing argument is anchored to one of a small number of forces investors recognize as durable, rather than a vague sense that "the market is moving." Naming the specific force, and only one usually needs to carry the argument, is what separates a structural shift from a marketing line.

Common miss:
Naming multiple forces vaguely ("regulation, changing behavior, and new AI tools are all converging") without evidence for any single one, which reads as hedging rather than a sharp argument.
What "done" looks like:
One force, named specifically, with a date: "A new disclosure rule takes effect in Q3 2027; 60% of our target customers have no compliant workflow yet." For a full breakdown of the three forces and how to evidence each, see The "why now" argument: proving your problem's timing at Series A.
3
Evidence the problem is growing, not shrinking, over time

This line is checking for a trend line, not a snapshot. A single strong data point about the problem's current size satisfies "the problem is real," but says nothing about direction. Investors are underwriting the next 3-5 years, so a flat or declining trend on the underlying problem undercuts everything else on the slide.

Common miss:
Reusing the same cost-per-customer figure from the Seed slide as if it were still current, with no comparison point showing whether it has moved.
What "done" looks like:
The same metric used to quantify the problem at Seed, tracked over 18-24 months: "cost per customer has grown from €X to €Y since [date]" or "the segment experiencing this problem grew from N to M over four quarters."
4
Framed as a window that is open now, not indefinitely

This is the line most founders skip entirely, because it asks you to argue against your own upside: that the opportunity will not stay wide open forever, and that funding you now is what lets you capture it before it closes. A timing argument without an expiry implicitly reads as "there's no rush," which undermines the urgency of the round itself.

Common miss:
Presenting the opportunity as permanently large and open, with no discussion of who else could close the window, or what happens if the round is delayed.
What "done" looks like:
A specific closing condition: "if we are not the category-defining tool for this within 18 months, a generalist platform absorbs it as a feature" or "the regulatory grace period ends in [date], after which early movers will already own the compliant customer relationships."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "This problem is more urgent than ever" (no distinct claim from Seed)
  • "Regulation, behavior, and technology are all shifting" (no single evidenced force)
  • Same cost figure as the Seed deck, no comparison over time
  • "This is a massive, growing opportunity" (no closing condition)
Actually done
  • "Window opened [date] because [specific trigger], still open because [specific trend]"
  • One named force, dated, with a source
  • "Cost per customer grew from €X to €Y over 18 months" (source cited)
  • "If we're not the category leader in 18 months, a generalist absorbs this as a feature"

The pattern across all four is the same: a specific claim, backed by a date or a trend, that could not have appeared unchanged on last round's slide. If a line could be copy-pasted from your Seed deck without editing, it is not yet doing the job the Series A checklist is asking of it.

A useful test

Read your problem slide out loud and ask: could this exact wording have been said a year ago, at Seed? If yes for any line, that line is restating the fundamentals, not making the timing argument this checklist requires.

Key takeaways
  • All four checklist lines test one thing: has the argument actually shifted from "the problem exists" to "the window is open now."
  • Naming one structural force with evidence beats gesturing at several without any.
  • "Growing, not shrinking" requires a trend over time, not a repeated snapshot from the Seed deck.
  • A timing argument needs a closing condition, what happens if the window shuts before you're funded.
  • If a line could be copy-pasted unedited from last round's deck, it isn't done yet.
