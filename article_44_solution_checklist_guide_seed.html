How to read the Seed Solution checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

By Seed, "we built something" isn't the bar anymore. The solution slide now has to show a shipped product, a real roadmap, a reason it's hard to copy, and proof it beats what customers were doing before. Four different claims, four different kinds of evidence.

The Seed solution checklist has four lines. Each pushes past what was acceptable at Pre-Seed: a rough demo becomes a live walkthrough, a features list becomes a defensibility argument. Here's what each line is really checking, the common shortcuts founders take, and what a complete answer looks like.

1
Live product walkthrough included: shipped version, not roadmap slides

This line checks that what's being shown is the production product customers actually use today, not a preview of what's coming. At Seed, investors expect a real login and a real workflow, and substituting future-state screenshots for the current build undercuts the traction claims made earlier in the deck.

Common miss:
Showing polished screenshots from an upcoming redesign instead of screen-recording the actual production app that paying customers log into today.
What "done" looks like:
"Recorded a 90-second walkthrough logged into Pathwell's live production account, showing a real clinic's referral queue with real, anonymized patient names blurred."
2
Core features and 12-month roadmap stated

This line checks whether the founder can separate what's built and load-bearing today from what's planned, with a believable, sequenced twelve-month view rather than an aspirational features list. The order matters as much as the items; a roadmap without prioritization reads as a brainstorm, not a plan.

Common miss:
Presenting a roadmap of fifteen features with no prioritization or stated reasoning for the order, making it read like a wishlist rather than a sequence of decisions.
What "done" looks like:
"Core today: referral intake, status tracking, fax-to-digital conversion. Next 12 months, in order: insurance eligibility check in Q1, EHR integration with two major platforms in Q2 and Q3, automated follow-up reminders in Q4."
3
Specific about what makes it defensible (tech, data, network effects)

This line forces the founder to name the actual mechanism behind their moat, whether that's proprietary data, a network effect, or a technical advantage, rather than asserting something vague like "we move faster" or "we have a great team," neither of which is a real defensibility argument.

Common miss:
Claiming "our AI matching is proprietary" without being able to say what data trains it or why a well-funded competitor couldn't replicate it within six months.
What "done" looks like:
"We've digitized 40,000 referral faxes across 60 clinics, the largest labeled dataset of PT referral routing in the Midwest. Our matching model improves with each clinic added; a new entrant starts at zero."
4
Why it's better than the current workaround, backed by evidence

This line checks that the comparison being made is against what customers actually do today, fax machines, spreadsheets, phone tag, not a hypothetical do-nothing scenario. The improvement claim needs a measured before-and-after, not an adjective like "10x better" with nothing underneath it.

Common miss:
Comparing the product to a vague, unnamed status quo instead of the real incumbent workaround customers were using, so the improvement claim has nothing concrete to be measured against.
What "done" looks like:
"Clinics using Pathwell reduced referral processing time from 4.2 days, fax and phone tag, to 6 hours, measured across 60 clinics over 90 days."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • Screenshots from an upcoming redesign shown instead of the live production app.
  • A 15-feature roadmap with no order or reasoning behind the sequence.
  • "Our AI matching is proprietary." (no data source, no explanation of why it's hard to copy)
  • "We're 10x better than the alternative." (no named workaround, no measurement)
Actually done
  • A 90-second walkthrough of the live production account, real clinic, real workflow.
  • "Core today: X, Y, Z. Next 12 months, in order: A (Q1), B (Q2-Q3), C (Q4)."
  • "40,000 digitized referrals across 60 clinics; the model improves with each clinic added."
  • "Processing time dropped from 4.2 days (fax and phone tag) to 6 hours, across 60 clinics."

The pattern across all four: at Seed, every claim on the solution slide needs a number or a named mechanism sitting underneath it. "Better," "proprietary," and "roadmap" are all words that sound like evidence until an investor asks the obvious follow-up question.

A useful test

For each claim on your solution slide, ask: if an investor asked "compared to what, measured how?" could I answer in one sentence with a number? If the answer is a shrug or an adjective, the claim isn't ready to be on the slide yet.

Key takeaways
  • Show the live production product, not previews of an upcoming redesign.
  • Sequence your 12-month roadmap with reasoning, not just a list of desired features.
  • Name the actual mechanism behind your defensibility, data, tech, or network effects, not vague claims.
  • Compare against the real workaround customers use today, with a measured before-and-after.
  • Every claim on the solution slide needs a number or a named mechanism underneath it.
