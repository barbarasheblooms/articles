Acquisition channels explained: the types, how they work, and what "good" looks like on each

SheBloomsGo-to-market

"We'll do content, paid, and partnerships" is not a channel strategy — it's a wish list. A real strategy starts with knowing what each channel actually is, how it behaves, and why it fits (or doesn't fit) your business.

An acquisition channel is any repeatable path through which a stranger becomes aware of your product and eventually becomes a customer. "Repeatable" is the key word — a single viral tweet or one lucky press mention is a moment, not a channel. Below are the channel families founders actually build businesses on, what each one is, and the shape of its economics.

Before the glossary
  • No channel is universally "best" — each one trades off cost, speed, and predictability differently, and the right mix depends on your price point, sales cycle, and how your buyers already search for solutions.
  • Channels compound differently: paid acquisition scales the moment you add budget but stops the moment you stop paying; organic channels (SEO, content, community) are slow to start but keep producing after the initial work.
  • Most durable businesses eventually run on 1-2 channels that work extremely well, not five that work adequately — but you rarely know which 1-2 until you've tested several.

Paid acquisition

Paid search SEM
Bidding on keywords (Google Ads and similar) so your product appears when someone actively searches for a solution to their problem.
Shape of the economics: high intent, immediately measurable, but cost rises as competitors bid up the same keywords. Works best when there's clear search demand for what you do — it doesn't create demand, it captures it.
Paid social
Ads on social platforms (Meta, LinkedIn, TikTok, and similar) targeting people by demographics, interests, or behavior rather than active search intent.
Shape of the economics: lower intent than search, so conversion rates are lower, but it's the only paid channel that can reach people who don't yet know they have the problem you solve. Costs scale with budget and are fully off the moment spend stops.

Organic & compounding channels

SEO & content marketing
Publishing content that ranks in search results for the questions and problems your buyers are already searching, so they find you without you paying per click.
Shape of the economics: slow to start (often 6-12 months to real traffic), but each piece of content keeps generating visits and leads long after it's published — the opposite cost curve from paid.
Product-led growth PLG
Letting the product itself drive acquisition — free trials, freemium tiers, or self-serve signup that let people experience value before ever talking to anyone on your team.
Shape of the economics: very low CAC per user once built, but only works if the product delivers value fast enough, with little to no onboarding, for someone to "get it" alone. Requires real product investment before it's a channel at all.
Referral & virality
Existing customers bringing in new ones, either organically (word of mouth) or through an engineered incentive (referral programs, invite mechanics).
Shape of the economics: the cheapest acquisition there is, but it's a multiplier on an existing channel, not a standalone one — it only produces volume once you already have customers who are happy enough to refer.
Community
Building or participating in spaces (Slack/Discord groups, forums, niche social communities) where your exact buyers already gather, and earning attention through genuine participation rather than ads.
Shape of the economics: very low direct cost but high time cost, and it's slow to compound — it works best as a trust-building layer underneath another primary channel, not as the main volume driver.

Relationship-driven channels

Outbound / sales-led
Your team proactively reaching out to prospects — cold email, cold calling, LinkedIn outreach — rather than waiting for them to find you.
Shape of the economics: expensive per lead (a rep's time and salary), so it only makes sense at higher price points where a single closed deal covers many hours of prospecting. Below roughly a few hundred dollars in contract value, the math rarely works.
Partnerships & channel sales
Selling through or alongside another company that already has a relationship with your target customers — integration partners, resellers, or co-marketing deals.
Shape of the economics: slow to set up (partnerships take months to negotiate) but each one can unlock a whole pool of pre-qualified customers at once, with a revenue share replacing a traditional CAC.
Events & conferences
Attending, sponsoring, or speaking at industry events where your buyers already congregate, either to generate leads directly or to build brand recognition.
Shape of the economics: high upfront cost per event with a lumpy, hard-to-attribute return — usually a channel businesses add once they have budget to spare, not one to bet an early-stage GTM on alone.
The question that actually picks your channel

Don't ask "which channel is best." Ask: how do my buyers behave today when they have this problem? If they already search for solutions, paid search or SEO fits. If they don't know they have the problem, paid social or content that creates awareness fits better. If the deal size is large and buyers expect a relationship, outbound or partnerships fit. The channel should match existing buyer behavior, not the tactic you find most exciting.

This overview is general and educational. Which channels are viable for your specific business depends on your unit economics, buyer behavior, and market — validate with real tests before committing budget at scale.
