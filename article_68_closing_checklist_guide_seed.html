How to read the Seed Closing checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

By Seed, the close has to do more work than "here's what we need." Investors are underwriting whether this founder sees far enough ahead to be worth backing twice.

The Seed closing checklist has four lines. Together they test whether the founder can hold a longer horizon than the current raise: the vision beyond the next milestone, the risks that could derail it, the people who've chosen to stand behind it, and the ask itself. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Long-term vision and path to Series A articulated

This line is checking whether the founder can connect today's traction to a specific Series A story, not just describe an ambitious future. A real answer names the metric threshold or proof point that would make the Series A case, not just a bigger version of the current pitch repeated with larger numbers.

Common miss:
Describing the long-term vision in inspirational terms ("we want to become the category leader") without naming what the Series A pitch will actually be built on.
What "done" looks like:
Imani Cole, founder of Rootwork, a supply chain visibility platform for regional food co-ops, states: "Our Series A story is 60 co-ops on the platform generating $1.8M ARR with under 3% monthly logo churn, proof that co-ops trust us with mission-critical inventory data."
2
Main risks acknowledged, with a plan for each

This line is verifying self-awareness paired with action, not just a list of things that could go wrong. Naming a risk without a mitigation reads as either naive or evasive; naming a risk with a specific, already-in-motion response signals a founder who thinks like an operator rather than a pitch-writer.

Common miss:
Listing generic startup risks ("competition," "market timing") without a specific plan attached to each, or without naming the risk that's actually most likely to hurt this company.
What "done" looks like:
"Our biggest risk is co-op procurement cycles running 4-6 months, slower than typical SaaS sales. We're mitigating by selling through 3 regional co-op associations who can bundle approvals across members at once."
3
Advisory board listed as a credibility signal

This line is checking whether the people around the founder actually reduce specific, identifiable risk for a Seed investor, not whether a list of names with logos exists. An advisor is a credibility signal when their expertise maps directly onto a gap in the founding team or a known weak point in the plan, not when they're simply well-known.

Common miss:
Listing advisors by name and past company alone, with no explanation of what specific gap they fill or what they've actually done for the company so far.
What "done" looks like:
"Dr. Elena Voss, former VP of Ops at a regional grocery cooperative network, joined as an advisor in March and personally introduced us to 4 of our first 12 co-op customers."
4
Raise amount and milestones this capital unlocks stated

This line is verifying that the ask, at Seed, is tied to the same Series A story the vision line already set up, closing the loop between what the founder wants and what they said they'd do with it. A raise amount without a named milestone leaves the investor to guess whether the money and the plan actually match.

Common miss:
Stating the raise amount and a runway figure, but not tying the milestone explicitly back to the Series A threshold named earlier in the deck.
What "done" looks like:
"Raising $2.1M to get from 18 to 60 co-ops and $1.8M ARR by month 20, the exact threshold we've outlined for our Series A conversation."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "We want to become the category leader" (no named Series A threshold)
  • "Competition and market timing are risks" (no specific plan for either)
  • Advisor names and logos listed with no stated gap they fill
  • "$2.1M raise" with no milestone tying it to the Series A story
Actually done
  • "Series A story: 60 co-ops, $1.8M ARR, under 3% monthly churn"
  • "Slow procurement cycles, mitigated via 3 regional associations"
  • "Advisor introduced 4 of our first 12 customers, fills ops gap"
  • "$2.1M to reach 60 co-ops, $1.8M ARR, by month 20"

The pattern across all four: at Seed, the close has to prove the founder is already building toward the round after this one, not just managing the round in front of them.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: does this connect back to the same Series A story, or does it stand alone? The vision, the risks and their mitigations, the advisors and the gaps they fill, and the raise itself should all point at the same numbered threshold, not four separate narratives.

Key takeaways
  • The long-term vision needs a named Series A metric threshold, not just an ambitious mission statement.
  • Risks should be specific to this company, each paired with a mitigation already in motion, not a generic startup-risk list.
  • Advisors count as a credibility signal only when they fill a named gap and have already done something measurable for the company.
  • The raise amount and its milestone should tie directly back to the same Series A threshold named earlier in the close.
  • Across all four lines, the goal is one continuous story pointing at the next round, not four disconnected claims.
