Vanity metrics vs. real traction: how to tell the difference before an investor does

SheBloomsTraction

A vanity metric is any number that goes up and to the right without telling you whether the business is actually working. Investors who see a lot of decks spot these fast — the goal is to spot them yourself first.

The test for any metric: does it change your understanding of whether customers are getting value and paying for it? If a number could go up while the business quietly fails, it's a vanity metric, or at least an incomplete one.

Common vanity metrics, and what to pair them with

Vanity metricWhy it misleads alonePair it with
Total signups / downloadsIncludes everyone who ever clicked once, including people who never returnedActive users, retention curve
Total waitlist sizeDoesn't show if these are your actual target customer or just curious peopleConversion rate, source of signups, growth rate
Page views / impressionsSays nothing about intent to buy or actual value deliveredClick-through to a real action, conversion rate
Total revenue ever earned (cumulative)Can look large while monthly revenue is flat or decliningCurrent MRR, MoM growth trend
Number of app installsDoesn't show if people use it more than onceDAU/MAU ratio, repeat usage rate
Social media followersRarely converts directly to paying customers, and can be bought or bot-inflatedTraffic or leads actually generated from that channel

The context test

Almost any metric becomes meaningful with the right context, and almost any metric becomes vanity without it. "10,000 signups" is vanity. "10,000 signups, of which 60% activated within a week and 30% are still active at 90 days" is real traction — same starting number, completely different story.

Worked example
A founder's first draft slide said: "50,000 app downloads in 3 months." An investor asked how many were still using it — the honest answer was under 2%. The revised slide instead led with: "1,100 weekly active users, growing 12% week-over-week, with 35% of new users returning in their second week" — a smaller headline number, but one that actually describes a functioning product, and one that survives scrutiny.
Leading with a vanity metric doesn't just fail to convince — it actively damages trust

An investor who catches a vanity metric being used to obscure a weak underlying number will scrutinize every other number on your slide harder, not less. It's better to lead with a smaller, honest, well-contextualized number than a large, hollow one.

A quick self-check before you finalize a traction slide
  • Could this number go up while the underlying business quietly gets worse? If yes, pair it with the metric that would catch that.
  • Am I reporting a cumulative total instead of a current or trending figure? Cumulative totals almost always look better than they should.
  • Have I filtered for the actual target customer, or does this number include a broader audience that wouldn't ever buy?
  • Would this number survive an investor asking "and how many of those are still active/paying today?"

Some metrics are genuinely useful as secondary or supporting evidence even if they're not the headline number — the mistake is leading with them or presenting them without the pairing metric that gives them meaning.
