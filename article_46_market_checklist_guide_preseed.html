How to read the Pre-Seed Market checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Pre-Seed, the Market checklist is where founders reach for the biggest number they can defend, and reviewers reach for the smallest reason to distrust it.

The Pre-Seed Market checklist has four lines. Together they're testing whether you understand the actual buyer in front of you well enough to size a market from the ground up, rather than starting from a market report and working backward to a story. This guide walks through all four: what each line is really verifying, the common way founders half-satisfy it, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
ICP defined specifically

This line is checking whether you can describe your buyer in a way that would let someone else go find ten more of them tomorrow. A specific ICP names the role, the company or household context, the trigger event that creates urgency, and how they currently cope without you. "Small business owners" or "busy parents" isn't a definition, it's a demographic, and demographics don't buy software or products, specific people with specific triggers do.

Common miss:
Describing the ICP by firmographics alone ("B2B SaaS companies, 10-50 employees") with no mention of the role, the trigger, or the current workaround, which makes it impossible to know who to actually go sell to next.
What "done" looks like:
"Solo bookkeepers serving 15-30 small business clients, who hit a wall around client 20 when spreadsheet reconciliation stops scaling and they start missing month-end deadlines."
2
TAM, SAM and SOM calculated bottom-up from your ICP

This line is checking your math, but more importantly it's checking your starting point. A bottom-up sizing starts with a real, countable unit ("there are approximately X people with this exact ICP") and multiplies by a price you can defend, rather than starting with a headline industry figure and carving off a percentage. The top-down shortcut is the single fastest way to lose credibility on this slide, because reviewers can smell a borrowed 1% instantly.

Common miss:
Citing a $40B industry report figure, then writing "if we capture just 1% of this market" with no connection back to the ICP count or the price point actually being charged.
What "done" looks like:
"~180,000 solo bookkeepers in the US (SAM, per state licensing board counts) x $588/year subscription = $106M SAM, with a $4M SOM based on capturing 3,700 bookkeepers via accountant referral in year three."
3
Main competitors named

This line is testing market awareness, not competitive strategy. At Pre-Seed, reviewers aren't expecting a moat, they're checking that you've actually looked and can name who your ICP is choosing today, including indirect alternatives like spreadsheets, a part-time hire, or doing nothing. Naming zero competitors reads as naivety, not as evidence of a blue ocean.

Common miss:
Writing "no direct competitors" and stopping there, without naming the two or three tools or habits your ICP is actually using in place of a real solution today.
What "done" looks like:
"Direct: QuickBooks Ledger add-ons, Bench.co (managed service, 3x our price). Indirect: Google Sheets templates, which 60% of interviewed bookkeepers still use as their primary system."
4
Market timing argument included

This line is checking whether you understand why this market is winnable now rather than five years ago or five years from now. A real timing argument points to a specific shift, regulatory, technological, behavioral, or cost-related, that changes what's possible today. Without it, a reviewer has no reason to believe the window is open rather than closed or not yet arrived.

Common miss:
A generic claim like "more people are going digital" with no specific event, deadline, or shift that explains why the timing is right for this exact ICP right now.
What "done" looks like:
"New IRS e-file thresholds taking effect in 2026 push another 400,000 sole proprietors into needing digital bookkeeping records for the first time, right as QuickBooks raises entry pricing 30%."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Small business owners" (a demographic, not a buyer with a trigger)
  • "1% of a $40B market" (top-down, disconnected from the ICP count)
  • "No direct competitors" (stops looking instead of naming alternatives)
  • "The market is growing" (no specific shift or deadline named)
Actually done
  • "Solo bookkeepers who hit a wall around client 20" (role, trigger, workaround)
  • "180,000 ICP units x $588/year = $106M SAM" (bottom-up, tied to a countable ICP)
  • "QuickBooks add-ons, Bench.co, and Sheets templates" (direct and indirect named)
  • "New e-file thresholds in 2026 push 400K sole proprietors to digitize" (dated, specific)

The pattern across all four: a market slide earns trust when every number and every claim traces back to a countable, named group of real people, not to an industry report or a category-level generalization.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: does this trace back to a specific, countable group of people I could list by name or role, or does it trace back to a report I downloaded? If your ICP, your math, your competitor list, and your timing argument all point to the same narrow group, the slide holds together. If any one of them floats free of that group, a reviewer will notice the seam.

Key takeaways
  • An ICP is specific when it names a role, a trigger event, and a current workaround, not just a demographic.
  • Bottom-up sizing starts from a countable ICP and a real price, never from a borrowed industry percentage.
  • Naming zero competitors reads as naivety; name direct and indirect alternatives, including spreadsheets and doing nothing.
  • A timing argument needs a specific, dated shift, not a generic claim that the world is going digital.
  • Every claim on the slide should trace back to the same narrow, named group of real buyers.
