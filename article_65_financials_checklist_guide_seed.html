How to read the Seed Financials checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

By Seed, "we'll figure out the burn as we grow" stops working as an answer. Investors expect a founder who already knows the number and can tell you what changes it.

The Seed financials checklist has four lines. Together they ask a single underlying question: does this founder run the business by the numbers, or write the numbers to match the story? This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Current MRR and monthly burn stated

This line is checking whether the founder tracks two numbers together, not in isolation. MRR without burn tells you growth is happening; burn without MRR tells you money is leaving. Stated together, they reveal the actual trajectory of the business right now, which is what a Seed investor is underwriting.

Common miss:
Leading with a growth percentage ("MRR up 40% quarter over quarter") while leaving burn out of the same sentence, so the reader can't tell if growth is outrunning cash.
What "done" looks like:
Dana Whitfield, founder of Fieldnote, a note-taking tool for agronomists, states: "$18,200 MRR as of July 2026, monthly burn of $31,000, meaning we're spending $1.70 for every $1 of recurring revenue we collect."
2
12-24 month financial projection included

This line is verifying that the founder has thought past the current month into a specific, numbered future, not that the projection will be accurate. It should show month-by-month or quarter-by-quarter figures for revenue, costs, and headcount, built from stated assumptions the reader can trace, not a smooth curve pasted in from a template.

Common miss:
Showing only a single end-state number ("$1.2M ARR by month 24") with no path or monthly detail connecting today to that figure.
What "done" looks like:
"18-month model: MRR grows from $18,200 to $95,000 by adding 14 new mid-market clients per quarter at a $2,100 average contract value, headcount grows from 6 to 11."
3
Path to break-even shown

This line is checking whether the founder has identified the specific point, in a month and a revenue figure, where the company stops needing outside capital to survive, and what has to be true to get there. It's not asking for a promise of profitability, it's asking for the mechanics behind that promise.

Common miss:
Stating break-even as a vague aspiration ("we expect to be profitable within two years") with no revenue or cost figure attached to the claim.
What "done" looks like:
"Break-even at $62,000 MRR, projected month 19, requiring gross margin to hold at 74% and CAC payback to stay under 5 months as we scale outbound."
4
Allocation of the raise across team, marketing, and product stated

This line is verifying that the raise has a spending plan across functions, not just a total. Investors read the split as a signal of strategic priority: a company that puts 70% of a raise into marketing is telling a different story than one putting 70% into product, and the checklist wants that story stated on purpose rather than left implicit.

Common miss:
Giving a total raise figure with a single line like "mostly for hiring," leaving the actual split across team, marketing, and product unstated.
What "done" looks like:
"$1.4M raise: 55% team (3 engineers, 1 CS hire), 30% marketing (paid + content to lower CAC from $410 to $280), 15% product (integrations with 2 top-requested field tools)."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "MRR up 40% quarter over quarter" (burn left out of the sentence)
  • "$1.2M ARR by month 24" (no monthly path shown)
  • "Profitable within two years" (no revenue figure or margin assumption)
  • "Raise mostly goes to hiring" (no split across functions)
Actually done
  • "$18,200 MRR, $31,000 burn, spending $1.70 per $1 collected"
  • "MRR to $95,000 via 14 new clients/quarter at $2,100 ACV"
  • "Break-even at $62,000 MRR, month 19, margin held at 74%"
  • "$1.4M: 55% team, 30% marketing, 15% product, with reasons"

The pattern across all four: a healthy-sounding trend is not the same as a traceable number. Seed investors have seen enough decks to know the difference, and the checklist exists to force the traceable version onto the page.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: if I removed the adjectives and kept only the numbers, would the sentence still make sense on its own? MRR and burn stated together, a month-by-month projection, a break-even month with its margin assumption, and a raise split by function should all survive that test without needing extra framing.

Key takeaways
  • MRR and burn need to be stated in the same breath so the reader can see the actual trajectory, not just the growth headline.
  • A 12-24 month projection needs visible monthly or quarterly detail, not a single end-state number.
  • Break-even should be a specific month and revenue figure, tied to the margin or cost assumption behind it.
  • The raise allocation should be split by function with a stated reason, not summarized as "mostly hiring."
  • Across all four lines, a traceable number beats a healthy-sounding trend every time.
