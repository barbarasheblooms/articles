How to read the Seed Traction checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Seed, "we're growing" stops being an answer. The checklist wants the arithmetic behind the growth, not the adjective.

The Seed traction checklist has four lines, and all four assume you can produce numbers, not impressions. That's the shift from Pre-Seed: investors now expect a founder to know her own unit economics well enough to state them unprompted. This guide unpacks all four lines: what each is really verifying, the common way founders half-satisfy it, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Month-over-month growth calculated with real numbers

This line is checking whether you actually calculate your growth rate or just gesture at it. "Calculated" means a specific formula applied to specific months, not a chart that looks like it's going up. Investors want to see the math because the math reveals whether growth is accelerating, decelerating, or lumpy around one big customer.

Common miss:
Showing a revenue chart with an upward line and the label "growing 20% a month" with no underlying numbers a reader could check.
What "done" looks like:
Verve, a bookkeeping SaaS for freelance accountants, shows: "MRR went from $8,200 in March to $9,850 in April (+20.1%), then to $11,760 in May (+19.4%)," says founder Talia Brenner, with the formula shown on the slide.
2
Retention rate and churn stated

This line is verifying that growth isn't a leaky bucket: new customers can mask a business that's losing existing ones just as fast. Stating retention and churn together, as two separate numbers, shows you're tracking the full picture rather than only the flattering half.

Common miss:
Reporting only gross new signups or only retention ("92% of customers stay"), while leaving out the churn number that would let an investor sanity-check it.
What "done" looks like:
"Verve's monthly logo retention is 93%, meaning 7% monthly churn, calculated across our 64 active accounts as of May," Talia states, with the cohort table attached.
3
Engagement or recurrence data included

This line is checking whether customers are actually using the product, not just still paying for it. A customer who logs in weekly and a customer who forgot to cancel look identical on an MRR chart, so engagement data is what separates real usage from inertia.

Common miss:
Treating "still paying" as proof of engagement, with no login frequency, feature usage, or session data to back it up.
What "done" looks like:
"71% of Verve's paying accounts log in at least 3 times a week to reconcile transactions, and the average account has connected 2.4 bank feeds," Talia reports, pulled directly from product analytics.
4
CAC and LTV estimated, even roughly

This line is verifying that you understand the economics of acquiring a customer well enough to know whether the business can eventually fund its own growth. "Even roughly" means a defensible back-of-envelope estimate is acceptable, but it must show the inputs, not just a ratio pulled from nowhere.

Common miss:
Quoting an LTV:CAC ratio like "3:1" with no breakdown of what was spent, on what channel, or how lifetime value was projected.
What "done" looks like:
"We spent $2,400 on paid search and content last quarter and acquired 18 customers, for a CAC of $133. At $150/month average revenue and 7% monthly churn, estimated LTV is about $2,140, roughly a 16:1 ratio," Talia's deck shows, with the calculation visible.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Growing 20% month over month" (no underlying numbers shown)
  • "92% retention" with no churn figure or cohort attached
  • "Customers are sticky" with no login or usage data
  • "LTV:CAC is 3:1" with no spend, channel, or churn inputs shown
Actually done
  • "$8,200 to $9,850 to $11,760 MRR across March, April, May, with formula shown"
  • "93% monthly logo retention, 7% churn, across 64 active accounts"
  • "71% of accounts log in 3+ times a week, averaging 2.4 connected bank feeds"
  • "$133 CAC vs. ~$2,140 LTV, calculation and channel shown"

The pattern across all four: Seed investors are checking your math, not your optimism. A ratio without inputs is a marketing claim; a ratio with inputs is a data point they can stress-test themselves.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: if an investor asked "how did you get that number," could I answer in one sentence with a formula, a cohort, or a dataset? If the honest answer is "it just feels that way," the line isn't done yet.

Key takeaways
  • Show the formula and the raw monthly numbers behind any growth-rate claim.
  • Always pair retention with churn so investors see the full picture, not the flattering half.
  • Use real product data, like login frequency, to prove engagement beyond continued payment.
  • Estimate CAC and LTV with visible inputs, even if the estimate is rough.
  • At Seed, a number with a formula beats a bigger number with none.
