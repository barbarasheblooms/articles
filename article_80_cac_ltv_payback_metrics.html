How to calculate CAC, LTV:CAC, and payback period — with worked examples and benchmarks

SheBloomsGo-to-market

"Our CAC is going down" means nothing without knowing what's in the number, what it's being compared to, and over what period. Here's how to calculate the three numbers that actually tell you whether a channel is working.

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)

CAC is what it costs, on average, to acquire one paying customer.

Formula
CAC = Total sales & marketing spend in a period ÷ Number of new customers acquired in that period

What belongs in "total spend": ad spend, marketing team salaries and tools, sales team salaries and commissions, agency fees, and content production costs. Leaving out salaries and only counting ad spend is the most common way founders understate their real CAC.

Calculating CAC per channel

Formula
Channel CAC = Spend on that channel ÷ Customers acquired through that channel, in the same period

Shared costs (a marketing generalist's salary, a shared tool) get allocated across channels either by revenue share or by volume share — pick one method and use it consistently so channel comparisons stay fair over time.

Worked example
You spent €4,000 on paid search and closed 40 customers from it this month: Channel CAC = €4,000 ÷ 40 = €100. In the same month you spent €1,500 on content production (freelance writers, your time valued at a market rate) and it drove 10 customers: Channel CAC = €1,500 ÷ 10 = €150. Paid search looks cheaper today — but content's cost is front-loaded and the same articles will keep producing customers next month for close to €0 in new spend, while paid search costs €100 again for every new batch of 40.

Lifetime Value (LTV) and the LTV:CAC ratio

LTV estimates how much revenue (or gross margin) a customer generates over their whole relationship with you, not just their first purchase.

Simplified subscription formula
LTV = (Average revenue per customer per month × Gross margin %) ÷ Monthly churn rate
Worked example
€150/month average revenue, 80% gross margin, 4% monthly churn (a 25-month average customer lifetime): LTV = (€150 × 0.80) ÷ 0.04 = €120 ÷ 0.04 = €3,000.

The LTV:CAC ratio divides that number by what it cost to acquire the customer:

Worked example (continued)
If that customer's channel CAC was €100: LTV:CAC = €3,000 ÷ €100 = 30:1 — well above the 3:1 benchmark investors and operators generally consider healthy for sustainable growth.

CAC Payback Period

Payback period answers a different, equally important question: not "is this profitable eventually," but "how many months until I get my acquisition spend back in gross margin."

Formula
CAC Payback (months) = CAC ÷ (Average monthly revenue per customer × Gross margin %)
Worked example (continued)
CAC = €100, monthly revenue × margin = €150 × 0.80 = €120: Payback = €100 ÷ €120 = 0.83 months — under a month, extremely healthy.

Reading the two numbers together

MetricHealthy benchmarkWhat it tells you
LTV:CAC ratio3:1 or higher (5:1+ may mean you're underinvesting in growth)Whether the unit economics work at all, over the customer's full lifetime
CAC payback periodUnder 12 months (best-in-class under 6)How long your cash is tied up before a customer becomes cash-flow positive

A 4:1 LTV:CAC ratio that takes five years to pay back can be worse for a cash-constrained early-stage company than a 3:1 ratio that pays back in ten months — the ratio alone hides how long your money is at risk. Always report both numbers together, never the ratio alone.

Common mistake: comparing channels on CAC alone

A channel with a higher CAC but much higher LTV (say, an enterprise partnership channel) can be far more attractive than a cheap channel that brings in customers who churn fast. Always pair CAC with LTV and payback before deciding a channel "won."

These formulas are simplified for clarity and assume steady monthly revenue and churn. Businesses with variable pricing, usage-based revenue, or seasonal demand should adapt the LTV formula accordingly — work with whoever owns your financial model to calibrate it to your actual revenue pattern.
