The founder's cap table glossary: ownership, dilution, and the terms you'll be tested on

SheBloomsTeam & governance

Most founders don't lose ownership because they made a bad deal. They lose it because they didn't understand the term until it was already signed. This is the vocabulary a cap table actually runs on.

A cap table — short for capitalization table — is the single ledger that tracks who owns what percentage of your company, in what form, and under what conditions. Every time you issue founder shares, bring on a co-founder, grant options to an employee, take a SAFE, or close a priced round, the cap table is the document that has to reflect it accurately. Get it wrong early and the errors compound: a messy cap table is one of the most common reasons diligence drags on or a round falls apart.

The problem is that cap table language is dense and the terms interact with each other in ways that aren't obvious from the definitions alone. Below is the vocabulary in the order you'll actually need it, with the mechanics spelled out, not just the dictionary definition.

Before the glossary
  • A cap table is a living document — it should be updated the day an equity event happens, not reconstructed from memory before a raise.
  • Ownership percentage is only meaningful in context of the fully diluted share count, not the number of shares you hold in isolation.
  • Almost every term below exists because founders and investors need a shared, precise way to answer one question: "if the company were sold or wound down right now, who gets what, in what order?"

Ownership & share mechanics

Authorized shares
The total number of shares your company's charter allows it to ever issue. Most startups authorize a round number like 10,000,000 at incorporation — far more than they'll issue immediately.
Why it matters: you can't issue more shares than you've authorized without amending your charter, so this ceiling should be set high enough to cover founder shares, an option pool, and several future rounds.
Issued & outstanding shares
The subset of authorized shares that have actually been granted to someone — founders, employees, investors. This is your current share count, before accounting for options that haven't been exercised.
Why it matters: your ownership percentage today is issued shares you hold divided by total issued and outstanding shares — not divided by the authorized ceiling.
Fully diluted shares
Issued and outstanding shares, plus every share that could exist if all options were exercised, all the option pool were granted and exercised, and all convertible instruments (SAFEs, notes) converted to shares.
Why it matters: this is the denominator investors actually use. Your ownership on a fully diluted basis is almost always lower than your ownership on an issued-and-outstanding basis — sometimes by 15-25 percentage points once you count the option pool and converting SAFEs.
Common stock
The class of shares typically held by founders and employees. Common stock has no special financial rights — it's paid out after every other class in a sale or liquidation.
Why it matters: knowing you hold common (not preferred) shares tells you exactly where you sit in the payout order — last.
Preferred stock
The class of shares issued to investors in a priced round. Preferred stock carries contractual rights common stock doesn't have — most importantly a liquidation preference, and often board seats, pro-rata rights, and anti-dilution protection.
Why it matters: preferred shareholders get paid before common shareholders in an exit, which is why a "good" acquisition price on paper can still leave founders with very little if preferences stack up.

Dilution & funding mechanics

Dilution
The reduction in your ownership percentage that happens whenever new shares are issued — a funding round, an option pool top-up, a new co-founder grant. You don't lose shares; the total number of shares your slice is divided by simply grows.
Why it matters: dilution is normal and not inherently bad — a smaller percentage of a company that's worth much more is usually the better outcome. The goal isn't to avoid dilution; it's to know exactly how much you're taking on and why.
Pre-money / post-money valuation
Pre-money is what the company is valued at right before new money comes in. Post-money is pre-money plus the amount actually raised. A €4M raise on a €16M pre-money valuation gives you a €20M post-money valuation.
Why it matters: the investor's new ownership percentage = amount invested ÷ post-money valuation. In the example above, that's €4M ÷ €20M = 20%.
SAFE / convertible note
Instruments that let you raise money before agreeing on a valuation. Instead of buying shares today, the investor buys the right to receive shares later — usually at your next priced round, at a discount and/or subject to a valuation cap.
Why it matters: SAFEs and notes don't show up as shares on your cap table until they convert — which means an early cap table can understate how diluted the founders will actually be once every outstanding SAFE converts. Always model conversions before you quote your ownership percentage to anyone.
Valuation cap
The maximum valuation at which a SAFE or note converts to equity, regardless of what valuation you raise your next priced round at. It protects early investors from being diluted by their own early bet paying off.
Why it matters: if you raise several SAFEs at different caps, the earliest, lowest-cap investors end up owning more shares per dollar invested than later ones — model this before assuming everyone converts at the same rate.
Option pool
A block of shares set aside — but not yet granted to anyone — specifically to hire and retain employees with equity. Typically 10-15% of the fully diluted cap table at Seed and Series A.
Why it matters: most term sheets require the pool to be created or expanded before the round closes, which means the dilution from the pool comes out of the founders' side, not the new investor's — it's one of the most common places founders lose ownership without realizing it.
Pro-rata rights
The right (not obligation) for an existing investor to invest enough in a future round to maintain their current ownership percentage, rather than being diluted like everyone else.
Why it matters: pro-rata rights reduce how much of the new round is available to new investors, which is worth knowing when you're planning how a future round's ownership will be split.

Protecting the company & each other

Vesting
A schedule that determines when shares or options you've been granted actually become fully yours. The startup standard is four years, with shares vesting monthly or quarterly after an initial period.
Why it matters: vesting protects the company, not just the investor — it's what stops a co-founder who leaves after two months from walking away with a full, unearned stake.
Cliff
A waiting period — almost always one year — during which none of your granted equity vests. If you leave before the cliff, you leave with zero vested shares from that grant.
Why it matters: on a standard four-year vest with a one-year cliff, 25% vests all at once on the one-year anniversary, then the rest vests monthly for three more years.
Acceleration (single vs. double trigger)
A clause that speeds up vesting under specific conditions. Single trigger accelerates on one event (usually an acquisition). Double trigger requires two events — typically an acquisition and the person being let go within a set window afterward.
Why it matters: investors generally push back on single-trigger acceleration because it can make a company harder to sell — an acquirer inheriting a workforce that's already fully vested has less incentive to keep them.
83(b) election
A filing with the tax authority (in the US, the IRS) made within 30 days of receiving restricted stock, electing to pay tax on its value now — usually near zero at founding — instead of as it vests, when the value may be far higher.
Why it matters: miss the 30-day window and the election is permanently unavailable for that grant. It's one of the few cap-table-adjacent deadlines that cannot be fixed after the fact.
Liquidation preference
A right, usually held by preferred shareholders, to be paid a set multiple of their investment back before common shareholders receive anything in a sale or wind-down. A "1x non-participating" preference is the market standard.
Why it matters: stacked preferences from multiple rounds can mean a sale that sounds successful in headlines still returns very little to founders and employees holding common stock — always check where you sit in the stack.
The one habit that prevents most cap table disasters

Update the cap table the same week an equity event happens — not the week before a raise. Founders who reconstruct months of grants, SAFEs, and departures from memory right before diligence are the ones who find the expensive surprises.

Worked example
Two co-founders split 100% 60/40 at incorporation, both on standard 4-year/1-year-cliff vesting. Before raising, they carve out a 10% option pool for future hires — this dilutes both founders proportionally, leaving them at 54%/36% fully diluted, with the pool holding the remaining 10%. They then raise a €1.5M SAFE at a €7.5M cap. When it converts at the next priced round, the SAFE investor's ownership is calculated against the fully diluted share count at conversion — which is why founders who never modeled the SAFE's eventual conversion are often surprised by how much smaller their post-conversion percentage is than the number they'd been quoting themselves.

This glossary is educational and general in nature. Cap table structuring, vesting terms, and tax elections are legal and financial decisions — work with a startup lawyer and accountant before finalizing yours.
