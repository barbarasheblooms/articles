Common business model mistakes, and how to check your plan is coherent

A business model does not fail because one block is weak. It fails because the blocks contradict each other. Here is what that looks like, and how to catch it before an investor does.

01The mistakes that show up most often

1
Pricing with no evidence. The price on the slide has never been quoted to, let alone paid by, an actual customer. Investors can usually tell within one follow-up question.
2
Listing every possible revenue stream. "We will monetize through subscriptions, transaction fees, advertising, and data licensing" signals that no primary model has actually been chosen yet.
3
Unit economics that only work "at scale." CAC and LTV are presented as a future state with no bridge from today's real numbers to that future state.
4
Confusing users with payers. Especially common in two-sided or free-to-use products, where the model describes engagement metrics but never says clearly who actually pays and why.
5
Costs that do not match the activities. A cost structure that lists "engineering" and "marketing" without any visible connection to the Key Activities or Key Resources described earlier in the model.

02Why coherence matters more than any single number

An experienced investor rarely rejects a business model because one number looks weak in isolation, early numbers are almost always rough. What raises concern is when the blocks of the model do not agree with each other: a self-service Channel paired with a Customer Relationship that clearly depends on manual sales calls, or a Revenue Stream priced for a mass-market Customer Segment while the Cost Structure assumes an enterprise sales team. Each piece can sound fine on its own and still describe two different businesses.

A useful test: read your Customer Segment, Value Proposition, and Revenue Streams blocks out loud, back to back, as three sentences. If a stranger would ask "wait, how does that connect to that," the model is not yet coherent, regardless of how good any individual block sounds.

03A self-check before you pitch

Run through this before sharing your model

  • Can you name your primary revenue model in one sentence, without using the word "and" more than once?
  • Is at least one price point in your model something a real customer has actually paid, not just been quoted?
  • Does your Customer Relationship block match your Channels block, self-service claims should not sit next to a manual sales process?
  • Does your Cost Structure visibly connect to your Key Activities and Key Resources, not just list department names?
  • If you have more than one Revenue Stream, can you say which one is primary today, not which one you expect to matter eventually?

04Use the canvas to catch this early

Most of these mistakes are invisible when the business model lives only in your head or across scattered slides, because nothing forces the pieces to sit next to each other. None of this requires a perfect model. It requires a model where every block agrees with every other block about who your customer is, what they are paying for, and why that gets more efficient as you grow.
