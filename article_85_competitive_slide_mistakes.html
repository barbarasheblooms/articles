The 6 mistakes that sink a competitive slide, and what to do instead

SheBloomsCompetitive landscape

Investors see hundreds of competitive slides a year. Almost all the bad ones fail for one of the same six reasons.

1. Claiming "we have no competitors"
Every business competes with something — a direct rival, an adjacent tool being used as a workaround, or the customer simply doing nothing. Saying you have none signals you haven't looked, not that you've found whitespace.
Fix: List both direct and indirect competitors, including the status quo alternative (spreadsheets, manual process, "nothing").
2. Leading with generic adjectives instead of mechanisms
"Better UI," "we're passionate," "we use AI" — none of these are things a competitor can't also claim, and none explain why a customer actually chooses you.
Fix: Name a specific, evidenced mechanism from the 7 real moat categories (see the differentiation article) instead.
3. Using a feature-checkmark grid as the main slide
A table where you have more green checks than everyone else looks convincing for about as long as it takes a funded competitor to ship the missing feature. It also invites the obvious follow-up question about what happens when they catch up.
Fix: Use a 2x2 positioning map or a structural argument (data, distribution, partnerships) that doesn't reduce to a feature list.
4. Only listing direct competitors
Ignoring indirect alternatives and the status quo hides the real bar your product has to clear on price and convenience, and makes your market look artificially uncontested.
Fix: Explicitly include the "do nothing" or manual-workaround alternative — it's almost always your biggest real competitor early on.
5. Not addressing what happens if a competitor copies you
Investors will ask this whether or not you address it on the slide. Leaving it unanswered reads as either not having thought about it, or not having a good answer.
Fix: State directly why your advantage holds up even after a well-funded competitor notices and reacts — this is the single question that separates a real moat from a temporary lead.
6. Using the same differentiation argument at every stage
What counts as differentiation at Pre-Seed (a sharp product insight, founder-specific expertise) is not enough at Series A, where investors expect to see the advantage compounding with scale, not staying flat.
Fix: Revisit and upgrade your differentiation argument each round — Pre-Seed can lean on insight, Seed needs early evidence, Series A needs proof the moat strengthens as you grow.
Worked example
A founder's first draft slide said: "No direct competitors — closest is Excel." Investors pushed back immediately. The revised version listed two direct competitors, named Excel and a common CRM plug-in as the real indirect competitors most prospects were actually using, and led with a specific data advantage (a proprietary dataset that improves match accuracy with every transaction) instead of "easier to use." The second version generated far fewer skeptical follow-up questions.
The goal is credibility, not a perfect scorecard

A competitive slide that honestly names strong competitors and gives a specific, evidenced reason you win is more convincing than one that claims an uncontested market. Investors fund founders who understand the fight they're in.

This list reflects common patterns, not a universal rule — some categories genuinely have thinner competitive fields than others. The test is always whether your analysis is honest and specific, not whether it fits a template.
