How to read the Pre-Seed Team checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Pre-Seed, the team checklist looks like a formality, a slide with names and logos. It's actually the line investors reread most carefully, because at this stage the team is most of what they're actually funding.

The Pre-Seed team checklist has four lines. Each is designed to surface something founders would rather smooth over: unclear roles, an unconvincing reason it's you, an unfilled gap, or a thin advisor list padded for optics. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Each founder's role and background listed

This line is checking whether responsibilities are actually divided, or whether every founder is doing a bit of everything with no clear owner for the functions that matter most right now. A real answer names who owns product, who owns revenue, and what specific experience backs each of those claims, not just a job title.

Common miss:
Listing both founders as "Co-Founder" with overlapping, vague descriptions like "product and growth," leaving it unclear who actually decides what ships or who owns a customer conversation.
What "done" looks like:
Harvestly (a farm-to-restaurant produce marketplace) lists Dana Okafor, CEO, who ran supply chain operations for a regional restaurant distributor for 6 years, and Marcus Reyes, CTO, who built the routing engine at a last-mile delivery startup for 4 years, each named with a decision they currently own.
2
Founder-market fit explained

This is checking for a specific, earned reason this team has an edge on this exact problem, not general competence or passion. Founder-market fit is demonstrated through direct exposure to the problem (worked in the industry, lived the pain, built something adjacent), not asserted through enthusiasm.

Common miss:
"We're passionate about fixing the food supply chain" or "we've always loved solving hard logistics problems," which states interest without evidence of direct exposure to the specific problem.
What "done" looks like:
Dana spent 6 years negotiating produce contracts for 40+ restaurants and personally lost two supplier relationships to price volatility she couldn't forecast, which is the exact failure Harvestly's pricing tool now solves.
3
Co-founder or key role gaps acknowledged, with a plan to close them

This line is checking for self-awareness and a credible next step, not a complete team. Every Pre-Seed team has gaps; the question is whether the founders know exactly which one matters most right now and have a specific, timed plan to fill it, rather than pretending the gap doesn't exist.

Common miss:
Either omitting gaps entirely to appear complete, or naming a gap so vaguely ("we'll need to hire eventually") that it signals no real plan exists.
What "done" looks like:
Harvestly has no dedicated salesperson; Dana is currently closing all restaurant accounts herself, and the plan is to hire a founding account executive with prior food-distribution sales experience within 60 days of closing this round, funded directly from it.
4
Advisors or mentors listed, if any

This is checking whether advisors are doing real work for the company, or whether they're recognizable names attached for credibility with no actual involvement. A short list of advisors who are genuinely engaged says more than a long list of logos who took one coffee meeting.

Common miss:
Listing five advisors with impressive titles, none of whom have had more than a single introductory call with the founders.
What "done" looks like:
Harvestly lists one advisor, Priya Sethna, a former Sysco regional VP, who reviews pricing strategy monthly and made two of the founders' first five restaurant introductions.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Co-Founder, product and growth" (vague, overlapping, no clear owner of any decision)
  • "We're passionate about fixing the food supply chain" (interest stated, no direct exposure shown)
  • "We'll need to hire eventually" (no named gap, no timeline, no plan)
  • "5 advisors" with recognizable names and no described involvement (logos, not engagement)
Actually done
  • "Dana, CEO, owns pricing and restaurant accounts; Marcus, CTO, owns routing engine" (named owner per decision)
  • "Dana lost two supplier relationships to price volatility over 6 years, the exact problem we now solve" (direct, specific exposure)
  • "No dedicated salesperson; hiring a founding AE within 60 days of close, funded from this round" (named gap, timed plan)
  • "1 advisor, former Sysco regional VP, reviews pricing monthly and made 2 of our first 5 introductions" (real, described involvement)

The pattern across all four: specificity and honesty about what's missing read as stronger signals than a polished, complete-looking slide. Investors at Pre-Seed expect gaps; what they're evaluating is whether the founders see them clearly.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: could a stranger tell exactly who decides what, why this founder specifically has an edge, what's missing and by when it gets filled, and what an advisor has actually done for the company? If any answer is a title or a feeling rather than a fact, the line isn't done yet.

Key takeaways
  • Roles should name a specific decision each founder owns, not overlapping, generic titles.
  • Founder-market fit needs direct exposure to the problem, not stated passion or general competence.
  • Gaps should be named specifically, with a timed plan, not smoothed over or left vague.
  • A short list of genuinely engaged advisors beats a long list of recognizable names with no involvement.
  • Investors expect gaps at Pre-Seed; they're evaluating whether the founders see them clearly.
