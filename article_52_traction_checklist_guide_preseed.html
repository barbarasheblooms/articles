How to read the Pre-Seed Traction checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Pre-Seed, "traction" doesn't mean big numbers. It means proof that something real is happening between you and a customer, and that it's pointed in the right direction.

The Pre-Seed traction checklist has four lines. None of them require impressive scale, which is exactly the trap: founders read "small is fine" as "vague is fine" and skip the specifics that actually make small numbers credible. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders half-satisfy it, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
First paying customers and initial MRR stated, even if small

This line is checking whether real money has changed hands, not whether people are interested. Interest is cheap: sign-ups, waitlist adds, and "I'd definitely use this" all cost the customer nothing. A number attached to actual payment is the first evidence that the problem is worth paying to solve, and stating it as MRR (not a lump one-time figure) shows you understand the difference between a sale and a subscription.

Common miss:
Reporting "40 sign-ups" or "120 people on the waitlist" as if it were traction, or citing total revenue to date without saying how much of it is recurring versus a single invoice.
What "done" looks like:
"Ledgerly has 11 paying customers at $19/month, for $209 in MRR as of July 2026, all on recurring subscriptions, not one-time invoices," says founder Priya Nathan.
2
Qualitative feedback from early adopters included

This line is verifying that the payment isn't a fluke, that customers can explain in their own words what changed for them. Investors read qualitative feedback as a second, independent signal on top of the MRR number: it tells them whether people are paying because the product actually solved something, or because a friend asked them to try it.

Common miss:
Summarizing feedback as "customers love it" or "great response so far" with no direct quote, no name, and no description of what specifically changed in their behavior.
What "done" looks like:
Priya interviewed her first 8 customers after 30 days; 6 said they'd stopped using spreadsheets entirely. Electrician Dave Okoro told her: "I used to spend Sunday nights doing invoices. Now it's done before I leave the job site."
3
Trend direction shown (up, even if numbers are small)

This line is checking for a slope, not a size. A single number, however it's framed, tells an investor nothing about momentum: it could be the peak, the trough, or a one-off. Three or four data points across consecutive months turn a static fact into evidence of direction, which is what Pre-Seed investors are actually pricing.

Common miss:
Showing one current MRR figure on the slide with no history, so a reader can't tell whether it's climbing, flat, or already declining.
What "done" looks like:
Ledgerly's MRR chart: $0 in April, $57 in May, $114 in June, $209 in July, four straight months of growth even though the base is still tiny.
4
Testimonials, press, or case studies added if available

This line is checking for external validation, proof that someone other than the founder is willing to vouch for the product in public. "If available" means it isn't a hard gate at Pre-Seed, but it's also not an excuse to skip a testimonial that already exists and simply hasn't been written down.

Common miss:
Leaving the slide blank when a happy customer would gladly give a quote, or padding it with a vague LinkedIn comment instead of a named testimonial tied to a specific outcome.
What "done" looks like:
A one-paragraph case study slide: "How Dave Okoro Electric cut invoicing time from 3 hours a week to 20 minutes," with his name, his business, and a link to the 4.8-star review he left.

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "120 people on our waitlist" (interest, not payment)
  • "Customers love the product" (no name, no quote, no specifics)
  • "$209 in MRR" shown with no history (no way to tell direction)
  • Testimonials section left blank despite happy paying customers
Actually done
  • "11 paying customers, $209 MRR, all recurring subscriptions"
  • "6 of 8 customers said they stopped using spreadsheets entirely, per Dave Okoro"
  • "$0 to $209 MRR over 4 months: April, May, June, July"
  • Named case study with outcome, business, and a public review link

The pattern across all four: a small number told precisely beats a vague claim told confidently. A named customer, a dated chart, and a direct quote each do more to convince an investor than any amount of enthusiasm on the slide.

A useful test

For each of the four lines, ask: is this a fact I can point to, or a feeling I'm describing? Money, quotes, dates, and names are facts. "Strong interest," "great feedback," and "growing fast" are feelings dressed up as facts, and Pre-Seed investors have seen that costume before.

Key takeaways
  • State MRR precisely, and separate recurring revenue from one-time payments.
  • Attach a name and a direct quote to every claim of customer feedback.
  • Show at least three data points over time so direction is visible, not just size.
  • Add a testimonial, press mention, or case study whenever one genuinely exists.
  • Specificity, not scale, is what makes small early traction numbers credible.
