How to read the Pre-Seed Financials checklist: what each line is actually asking

By SheBlooms 5 min read

At Pre-Seed, the financials slide isn't judged on precision. It's judged on whether the numbers on it came from real arithmetic or from a benchmark deck you skimmed the night before.

The Pre-Seed financials checklist has four lines. None of them ask for a forecasting model an investor would trust on its own; all four ask whether you understand your own numbers well enough to defend them out loud. This guide unpacks all four: what the line is really verifying, the common way founders satisfy the letter of it without the substance, and what a genuinely complete answer looks like.

1
Raise amount and intended use of funds defined

This line is checking whether the raise amount was derived from a plan or picked from a range other founders quoted. A defined use of funds means the number breaks into categories tied to specific activities and timeframes, not a single line like "hiring and growth." An investor reading this wants to see that the ask is sized, not guessed.

Common miss:
Naming a round figure ("raising $400K for product and growth") with no breakdown, so the number can't be checked against anything.
What "done" looks like:
Priya Nair, founder of CribCare, a vetted-babysitter marketplace, is raising $350K: $180K for two part-time engineers over eight months, $90K for background-check and insurance compliance, $50K for founder salary, and $30K as buffer.
2
Runway calculated in months with this raise

This line is verifying that the founder can turn a raise amount into a burn rate and a timeline, not just quote a number that sounds standard. "12-18 months of runway" is not a calculation, it's a phrase. The math should be visible: raise divided by monthly burn, landing on a specific milestone the company reaches before the money runs out.

Common miss:
Stating a runway range that matches what other founders in the deck-writing guides used, without showing the monthly burn figure that produces it.
What "done" looks like:
"$350K raised divided by $19,400 average monthly burn gives us 18 months of runway, reaching 250 paying families by month 16, our target for the seed conversation."
3
Basic assumptions stress-tested (e.g., CAC at twice the expected cost)

This line is checking whether the model survives being wrong, not whether a model exists. Every early-stage forecast rests on assumptions that are more hope than data: CAC, conversion rate, sales cycle length. Stress-testing means running the same model with a pessimistic version of the key assumption and seeing what breaks, then stating the result.

Common miss:
Presenting a single base-case projection with no downside scenario, so the founder has never actually seen what happens if the main assumption is off.
What "done" looks like:
"Modeled CAC at $40 (expected) and re-ran it at $80. Runway drops from 18 to 14 months because paid acquisition is capped at 30% of new sign-ups, referrals cover the rest."
4
Prior capital raised, if any, disclosed

This line is verifying transparency about the cap table's history, not just its current state. A friends-and-family note, an accelerator check, or a convertible instrument from six months ago all affect how this raise sits on top of what already exists. Investors expect to see it stated with terms, not discovered later in diligence.

Common miss:
Leaving out a small friends-and-family round because it feels informal or too minor to mention, then having it surface as a surprise during diligence.
What "done" looks like:
"$40K raised from friends and family in January 2026 via an uncapped SAFE with a 20% discount, no other prior capital raised."

Checked the box vs. actually done

Reads as checked, isn't done
  • "Raising $400K for product and growth" (no breakdown by category)
  • "12-18 months of runway" (no burn rate shown to back it up)
  • Single base-case model with no downside scenario run
  • No mention of prior capital, assumed to mean none exists
Actually done
  • "$350K: $180K engineering, $90K compliance, $50K salary, $30K buffer"
  • "$350K / $19,400 monthly burn = 18 months, to 250 paying families"
  • "CAC at $80 (2x expected) still leaves 14 months of runway"
  • "$40K friends & family raised, uncapped SAFE, 20% discount"

The pattern across all four: a number without its arithmetic invites doubt, and a number with its arithmetic invites a real conversation. Investors aren't grading you on whether the forecast turns out to be right, they're grading whether you can explain how you got it.

A useful test

For each number on the financials slide, ask: can I show the calculation that produced it, live, without opening a spreadsheet? If the raise amount, the runway figure, the stress-tested case, or the prior capital line only exists as a stated fact and not a traceable calculation, it isn't ready yet.

Key takeaways
  • A raise amount is only credible when it breaks into categories tied to specific activities, not a single round figure.
  • Runway needs a visible calculation (raise divided by monthly burn) landing on a named milestone, not just a stated range.
  • Stress-testing means re-running the model with a pessimistic assumption and reporting what actually breaks.
  • Prior capital, even informal friends-and-family money, should be disclosed with terms rather than left to surface in diligence.
  • Across all four lines, a number backed by visible math beats a number that merely sounds standard.
